## Abstract

**Background:** Direct pharmacological inhibition of RAS, long dismissed as an undruggable target, has entered clinical oncology following the discovery of allosteric switch-II pockets and tri-complex molecular glues.

**Methods & Evidence Synthesis:** This review synthesises seven pivotal phase III randomised controlled trials across advanced non-small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, and *NRAS*-mutant melanoma, tracking efficacy endpoints, crossover mechanics, and class-specific toxicities.

**Key Findings:** Phase III trials consistently demonstrate meaningful gains in progression-free survival and objective response rates. However, overall survival benefits have been substantially confounded by post-progression crossover, achieving statistical significance only where crossover was strictly prohibited or where effective salvage therapy was unavailable. In colorectal cancer, rapid pathway reactivation through upstream receptor tyrosine kinases mandates vertical EGFR blockade, although targeted combinations have yet to surpass standard second-line chemotherapy. Safety profiles are dominated by low-grade rash, diarrhoea, and transaminase elevations; while grade >= 3 event rates remain comparable to cytotoxic comparators, cumulative toxicities require proactive surveillance.

**Conclusions:** The frontline migration of direct RAS inhibitors and adjuvant escalation will determine whether targeting RAS improves curative intent. Overcoming polyclonal resistance and establishing pre-treatment molecular stratification represent the core priorities for next-generation trials.

**Keywords:** RAS-targeted therapy; KRAS inhibitors; Randomised controlled trials; Drug resistance; Precision oncology

# 1. Introduction

RAS proto-oncogenes represent the most frequently mutated oncogene family in human solid tumours, yet their clinical prevalence exhibits marked tissue-specific divergence. In The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) pan-cancer cohort, 10.7% of resected primary tumours harbour non-synonymous mutations in *KRAS*, *NRAS*, or *HRAS*; in large clinical sequencing registries encompassing advanced and metastatic disease, this proportion approaches 19% [1,2]. Oncogenic alterations cluster densely within pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, colorectal cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, and cutaneous melanoma, whereas they remain vanishingly rare in breast carcinoma, prostate adenocarcinoma, and glioblastoma multiforme [2,3]. In 2021, the US Food and Drug Administration granted accelerated approval to sotorasib—the first small molecule directly engaging a RAS protein—marking the end of four decades during which the GTPase was deemed clinically undruggable [4]. Over the subsequent five years, multiple phase III randomised controlled trials have evaluated direct RAS and downstream pathway inhibitors. Global regulatory outcomes, however, have diverged sharply: certain agents converted smoothly to standard marketing authorisation, others remain under accelerated approval review, and selected indications were rescinded following confirmatory trial readouts.

This review addresses what these seven pivotal randomised trials establish and what critical uncertainties they leave open. In oncology drug development, randomised controlled trials provide the definitive benchmark for determining whether an investigational regimen improves clinical outcomes relative to standard care; single-arm cohorts and early-phase dose-expansion studies establish antitumour activity but cannot resolve comparative clinical efficacy. Accordingly, this article centers on seven landmark phase III randomised trials, systematically dissecting their baseline characteristics, comparator stringency, endpoint decoupling, and underlying clinical causality. The analysis focuses primarily on compounds that directly bind RAS, encompassing GDP-bound covalent G12C inhibitors, non-covalent allele-selective compounds, and tri-complex molecular glues targeting the GTP-bound active state. Upstream and parallel pathway inhibitors are incorporated to contextualise adaptive resistance or delineate biological boundaries, with MEK inhibition evaluated as a historical benchmark. The clinical evidence spans non-small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, and cutaneous melanoma, with literature cut-off through November 2026.

The review begins by tracing the structural biology of RAS, the mechanics of GTPase cycling, and the molecular epidemiology underpinning allele distribution across organs. It then examines the biophysical properties that hindered direct targeting and categorises the four mechanistic drug classes that emerged after 2013. Next, the clinical evidence from the seven randomised trials is evaluated by tumour type, focusing on the causal mechanisms driving endpoint divergence and cumulative toxicity profiles. Finally, the analysis explores ongoing frontline combination regimens, adjuvant escalation in resected disease, polyclonal resistance patterns, and the biomarker hurdles that must be resolved to guide patient stratification.

# 2. Structural biology, GTPase cycling, and molecular epidemiology of RAS

## 2.1 Protein structure, GTPase cycling, and downstream effector networks

RAS proteins function as binary molecular switches whose conformational transitions are strictly governed by guanine nucleotide exchange factors and GTPase-activating proteins. The canonical human RAS proto-oncogene family comprises three genes—*KRAS*, *NRAS*, and *HRAS*—encoding highly conserved 21-kDa small GTPases [5]. Each isoform contains a catalytic G-domain composed of an effector lobe (residues 1-86) that recruits regulators and downstream transducers, and an allosteric lobe (residues 87-166) that modulates dimerisation and membrane orientation; a flexible C-terminal hypervariable region undergoes post-translational lipid modification to anchor the protein to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane [5,6]. RAS cycles between an inactive guanosine diphosphate (GDP)-bound conformation and an active guanosine triphosphate (GTP)-bound state [5,7]. Upon GTP binding, two dynamic structural loops—switch I (residues 30-38) and switch II (residues 60-76)—contract tightly to form the primary binding interface for effector proteins; guanine nucleotide exchange factors (such as SOS1) accelerate signal onset by facilitating GDP dissociation and enabling replacement by abundant cytosolic GTP, whereas intrinsic GTP hydrolysis is sluggish and relies on GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) inserting an 'arginine finger' to coordinate with residue Gln61 for catalytic nucleotide cleavage back to the GDP-bound resting state [5,8,9].

In its GTP-bound state, RAS coordinates downstream oncogenic signalling through two primary effector cascades while remaining subject to endogenous negative feedback control. These pathways comprise the RAF-MEK-ERK (MAPK) kinase cascade, which orchestrates cell-cycle progression and transcriptional activation, and the PI3K-AKT-mTOR cascade, which curbs apoptosis and drives metabolic reprogramming (Figure 1a) [5,10,11]. Under physiological conditions, active ERK imposes feedback phosphorylation onto upstream receptor tyrosine kinases, SOS1, and RAF, preventing signal escalation (Figure 1a, red dashed lines) [11,12]. Oncogenic mutations abrogate intrinsic GTPase activity or shield the catalytic site from GAP-mediated hydrolysis, trapping RAS in a persistent GTP-bound conformation. This deregulation causes sustained downstream flux, metabolic reprogramming, accelerated macropinocytosis, enhanced autophagy, establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and bypass of cellular senescence (Figure 1b) [5,9,11].

## 2.2 Molecular epidemiology and allele-specific landscape across solid tumours

Sequencing registry composition and tissue sampling timing directly govern observed RAS mutation frequencies. In the TCGA whole-exome sequencing cohort, which comprises 10,967 surgically resected primary specimens across 32 tumour types, the non-synonymous RAS mutation rate is 10.7%; conversely, clinical sequencing cohorts focused on advanced and metastatic cohorts (such as the MSK-IMPACT series of 10,945 patients, Figure 2) document prevalence rates near 19% [1,2]. In colorectal cancer, for example, the COSMIC registry of approximately 75,000 specimens reports a 33% *KRAS* mutation frequency, whereas the Foundation Medicine registry of 13,336 metastatic biopsies records a 50% frequency [2]. Mutation frequencies peak in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, colorectal cancer, cutaneous melanoma, and lung adenocarcinoma, while remaining scarce in breast cancer (1.4%), prostate adenocarcinoma (1.2%), and glioblastoma multiforme (1.0%) [1]. Comparing mutation frequencies across series requires standardising clinical stage, tissue acquisition timing, and sequencing platform coverage [1,2].

Oncogenic RAS mutations display striking tissue tropism and distinct allele preferences across anatomical sites. Among all oncogenic RAS mutations, *KRAS* accounts for 75%, *NRAS* for 17%, and *HRAS* for 7% [2]. Across 29 cancer types with adequate statistical power, 19 are dominated by *KRAS* alterations. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, *KRAS* mutations occur in 88%–93% of tumours, predominantly G12D (40%–45%), G12V (~30%), and G12R (~15%), whereas G12C accounts for less than 2%; clinicopathologically, pancreatic ductal tumours exhibit dense desmoplastic, hypovascular stroma, intense perineural invasion, and early systemic seeding, with higher mutant allele copy numbers correlating with poorer overall survival [13,14]. Colorectal tumours exhibit *KRAS* alterations in 40%–45% of cases, primarily G12D, G12V, and G13D, with G12C found in 3%–4% [15]. Crucially, *KRAS* mutations in colorectal cancer are significantly enriched in right-sided (proximal) colon tumours arising from the embryonic midgut, which clinically correlate with older age, female predominance, mucinous differentiation, advanced presentation, and inferior post-progression survival compared with left-sided wild-type tumours [15,16]. Activating mutations in *KRAS* or *NRAS* exons 2, 3, or 4 sustain receptor-independent MAPK signaling, establishing de novo resistance to anti-EGFR antibodies (cetuximab, panitumumab) and forming an absolute contraindication in clinical practice guidelines [17,18]. By contrast, *NRAS* alterations are prominent in cutaneous melanoma (17.5%, mostly Q61R/K/L) and haematological malignancies, whereas *HRAS* mutations cluster within non-melanoma skin cancers, urothelial bladder carcinomas, and salivary gland tumours [2,3].

In lung adenocarcinoma, tobacco carcinogen exposure and distinct mutagenic processes shape a highly segregated clinicopathologic spectrum. In TCGA, *KRAS* mutations occur in 29.7% of lung adenocarcinomas compared with 1.4% of lung squamous cell carcinomas [1]. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in cigarette smoke induce bulky DNA adducts and stereotypic G:C>T:A transversions, enriching *KRAS* G12C mutations to approximately 40% of *KRAS* alterations in heavy smokers (median >30 pack-years); conversely, spontaneous or aging-associated deamination produces G:C>A:T transitions, enriching *KRAS* G12D (~15%) and G12V (~15%) primarily among never- or light-smokers [19–21]. Comprehensive multi-institutional cohorts demonstrate that *KRAS* G12D lung cancers represent a clinically and biologically distinct entity compared with non-G12D (predominantly G12C) tumours: G12D tumours are significantly enriched in never-smokers (22% vs 5%, P<0.0001), exhibit lower pack-year smoking exposure, significantly lower tumour mutational burden (median 8.4 vs 9.9 mut/Mb, P<0.0001), lower PD-L1 tumour proportion scores (median 1% vs 5%, P<0.01), and diminished intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration [21]. Furthermore, *KRAS* alterations heavily cluster within invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (IMA) of the lung (~60%–70%, predominantly G12D and G12V), a morphologically distinct subtype characterized by columnar goblet-cell architecture, peripheral spread, lack of thyroid transcription factor 1 (NKX2-1/TTF-1), aberrant hepatic nuclear factor 4-alpha (HNF4A) expression, and marked intrinsic resistance to cytotoxic chemotherapy and targeted inhibitors [21,22]. In East Asian populations, distinct smoking patterns and genomic architecture result in an overall *KRAS* mutation frequency of 8%–13%, with G12C representing roughly 30% of mutations [19,20].

The clinical behavior and therapeutic vulnerability of *KRAS*-driven tumours are decisively shaped by co-occurring genomic alterations. In lung adenocarcinoma, the seminal Skoulidis classification delineates three biologically and immunologically distinct subsets: KP (*KRAS* + *TP53*, ~40%), KL (*KRAS* + *STK11/LKB1*, ~30%), and KC (*KRAS* + *CDKN2A/B*, ~15%) [23,24]. KP tumours occur predominantly in smokers, harbour elevated somatic mutation burdens, express robust PD-L1, and display an inflamed, T-cell-influxed immune microenvironment that translates into superior response rates and durable survival benefits with immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) monotherapy [23,25]. Conversely, KL tumours lose the master metabolic kinase AMPK, driving downstream STING-TBK1 pathway repression, functional T-cell exclusion, and dense neutrophil infiltration, which generates an immunologically 'cold' microenvironment and profound primary resistance to anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy [23,24,26]. Concomitant inactivation of *KEAP1* (frequently overlapping with *STK11*) constitutively stabilizes NRF2, driving high-capacity antioxidant and xenobiotic detoxification that confers broad chemo-, immuno-, and targeted resistance [24,26]. These genomic and phenotypic divergences dismantle the historical assumption that *KRAS* mutation status serves as a uniform biomarker of immunotherapy sensitivity. While *KRAS*-mutant lung tumours historically benefited from ICIs due to smoking-induced neoantigen loads, clinical benefit is strictly partitioned by allele and co-mutation: *KRAS* G12D lung cancers experience significantly inferior progression-free survival (HR 1.51, P=0.003) and overall survival (HR 1.45, P=0.02) to anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy compared with non-G12D cohorts, mandating chemo-immunotherapy combinations to overcome immune desertification [21]. In gastrointestinal malignancies, over 95% of *KRAS*-mutant pancreatic and colorectal tumours are microsatellite-stable (MSS/pMMR), embedded within dense, immunosuppressive desmoplastic stroma that renders checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy virtually ineffective [13,15].

# 3. Biophysical barriers and direct pharmacological targeting strategies

## 3.1 Overcoming the 'undruggable' paradigm: discovery of the switch-II pocket

For nearly forty years, direct pharmacological inhibition of RAS was blocked by picomolar nucleotide binding affinities and a lack of tractable hydrophobic surface pockets. The dissociation constants (Kd) for GDP and GTP range between 10^-11 and 10^-12 M, whereas physiological intracellular concentrations of GTP (~0.5 mM) and GDP (~0.05 mM) are high; small-molecule competitive antagonists at micromolar concentrations cannot displace native nucleotides [27,28]. Compounding these affinity constraints, the catalytic G-domain lacks the deep hydrophobic binding clefts characteristic of protein kinases, displaying a relatively smooth surface with dispersed charges and dynamic conformational plasticity [27,29].

Early therapeutic strategies that bypassed direct nucleotide competition failed to deliver meaningful clinical benefit. Farnesyltransferase inhibitors (such as tipifarnib) sought to block post-translational CAAX lipid modification and displace RAS from the cell membrane; however, KRAS and NRAS utilised alternative prenylation via geranylgeranyltransferase-I to sustain membrane localisation, leading to negative phase III trials in pancreatic cancer [30–32]. The strategy showed activity only in the uncommon subset of head and neck squamous cell carcinomas driven exclusively by mutated *HRAS*, which lacks this bypass mechanism [32]. Directly blocking downstream MEK kinases (trametinib, selumetinib) relieved ERK-mediated feedback inhibition onto upstream receptor tyrosine kinases, triggering rapid pathway rebound and compounding gastrointestinal and cutaneous toxicities when combined with chemotherapy, yielding no survival gain in pretreated NSCLC randomised trials [33,34]. In parallel, synthetic lethal screening candidates (such as STK33) failed reproducibility testing in subsequent validation studies [35].

The identification of the switch-II allosteric pocket in 2013 provided the foundational biophysical breakthrough for direct RAS targeting. Ostrem and Shokat resolved crystal structures of GDP-bound KRAS G12C, uncovering an inducible, shallow hydrophobic cavity (switch-II pocket, S-IIP) situated between switch II and the central beta-sheet (beta1-beta3, Figure 3a,b) [27,36]. This pocket is accessible only when the protein occupies the inactive GDP-bound state, collapsing when switch II tightens in the active GTP-bound conformation [36,37]. Small molecules bearing electrophilic acrylamide warheads form an irreversible covalent adduct with the nucleophilic thiol of mutant Cys12, trapping KRAS in its inactive GDP-bound state [36,38]. Inhibitor binding shifts switch II outward by approximately 4.8 A, disrupting the effector-binding interface and impeding GEF-mediated nucleotide exchange (Figure 3b) [39]. By exploiting the residual intrinsic GTPase activity that transiently returns mutant KRAS to its GDP-bound state, covalent inhibitors establish allele-specific allosteric trapping without disrupting wild-type RAS cycling [36,40,41].

## 3.2 Four contemporary mechanistic classes of direct RAS inhibitors

Direct RAS drug development has expanded into four distinct mechanistic classes defined by conformational state, chemical engagement, and allele selectivity (Table 1). Class I consists of covalent G12C inhibitors that bind the inactive GDP-bound conformation. Class II encompasses non-covalent inhibitors targeting non-cysteine alleles or broad KRAS variants in the GDP state. Class III features tri-complex molecular glues capable of engaging the active GTP-bound state. Class IV comprises targeted protein degraders that direct RAS to the proteasome. Each class exhibits distinct clinical utility, combination partners, and resistance mechanisms.

Class I covalent G12C inhibitors require the protein to cycle into the GDP-bound state, displaying fine conformational differences in allele cross-reactivity and residue engagement. First-generation inhibitors sotorasib and adagrasib secured regulatory approvals for pretreated advanced NSCLC in 2021 and 2022 [4,42]. Both agents bind exclusively to GDP-bound KRAS, leaving the active GTP pool uninhibited [42]. Structurally, sotorasib avoids contact with His95, conferring cross-reactivity toward NRAS G12C [43]; adagrasib and the second-generation inhibitor divarasib form a hydrogen bond with KRAS-specific His95, restricting their activity to KRAS G12C and yielding 5- to 20-fold higher in vitro potency for divarasib [44,45]. Chinese-developed covalent inhibitors—including glecirasib, garsorasib, and fulzerasib—have similarly earned conditional approvals based on registrational phase II trials in NSCLC [46–48].

Class II non-covalent inhibitors target alleles lacking nucleophilic cysteines by establishing extensive non-covalent contact networks. *KRAS* G12D accounts for 27% of all *KRAS* mutations and 46% of pancreatic cancer alterations [3,14]. Because the aspartate carboxylate group lacks the reactivity required for covalent bond formation, inhibitors rely on electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions. MRTX1133 incorporates a positively charged pyrrolidine ring that forms a salt bridge with Asp12 while packing tightly into the switch-II cavity, achieving sub-nanomolar affinity for KRAS G12D [49]. Pan-KRAS inhibitors (such as BI 2865 and BI 3706674) exploit conserved residues within the switch pocket to engage multiple GDP-bound mutant isoforms alongside wild-type KRAS [40,50].

Class III tri-complex molecular glues bind the active GTP-bound conformation, overcoming the physical barrier of an otherwise closed switch-II pocket. These small molecules first bind the abundant chaperone cyclophilin A (CYPA), creating a composite binary interface; this complex then docks onto the effector-binding surface of GTP-bound RAS, forming a stable CYPA-drug-RAS tri-complex [51,52]. The steric bulk of CYPA physically occludes the recruitment of RAF, PI3K, and other effectors, halting signal propagation [53]. Daraxonrasib (RMC-6236) acts as a multi-RAS(ON) inhibitor with nanomolar affinity across GTP-bound KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS [54]. Allele-selective molecular glues (such as RMC-6291 for G12C and RMC-9805 for G12D) pair mutant-specific pocket binding with chaperone-mediated steric hindrance [50,55].

Class IV targeted protein degraders remove both the catalytic activity and the physical scaffolding functions of RAS. Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) use bifunctional molecules to tether target RAS proteins to E3 ubiquitin ligases (such as VHL or CRBN), triggering polyubiquitination and 26S proteasomal degradation [56,57]. Molecular glue degraders (such as ASP3082) induce neo-substrate interactions to degrade KRAS G12D [58]. By removing the protein entirely, degraders eliminate scaffolding interactions that contribute to pathway reactivation and drug resistance [56,58].

Seven pivotal phase III randomised controlled trials across four tumour indications define the clinical evidence for RAS and pathway inhibitors (Table 2). In NSCLC, three trials evaluated covalent G12C inhibitors against docetaxel or head-to-head against a first-generation agent. In colorectal cancer, two trials tested vertical EGFR combination regimens against chemotherapy or standard care. In pancreatic cancer and melanoma, individual phase III trials evaluated an active-state molecular glue and downstream MEK inhibition, respectively. # 4. Deep clinical logic and evidence boundaries established by pivotal phase III randomised trials

Seven pivotal phase III randomised controlled trials across four tumour indications define the clinical evidence for RAS and pathway inhibitors (Table 2). In NSCLC, three trials evaluated covalent G12C inhibitors against docetaxel or head-to-head against a first-generation agent. In colorectal cancer, two trials tested vertical EGFR combination regimens against chemotherapy or standard care. In pancreatic cancer and melanoma, individual phase III trials evaluated an active-state molecular glue and downstream MEK inhibition, respectively. Table 2 summarises study designs, primary endpoints, and clinical conclusions across these studies.

## 4.1 Advanced non-small cell lung cancer: chemotherapy comparators and head-to-head evolution

The phase III CodeBreaK 200 trial established that covalent G12C inhibition provides superior progression-free survival (PFS) and health-related quality of life compared with standard second-line chemotherapy, though a mid-trial sample size reduction constrained overall survival analyses. The trial enrolled 345 patients with advanced *KRAS* G12C-mutated NSCLC previously treated with platinum-based chemotherapy and PD-(L)1 inhibitors, randomising them 1:1 to sotorasib (960 mg once daily) or docetaxel (75 mg/m2 every three weeks) [59]. At a median follow-up of 17.7 months, blinded independent central review demonstrated a median PFS of 5.6 months with sotorasib versus 4.5 months with docetaxel (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.51-0.86, P=0.0017) [59]. The 12-month PFS rate was more than double in the sotorasib group (24.8% vs 10.1%), with objective response rates (ORR, 28.1% vs 13.2%, P<0.001) and disease control rates (82.5% vs 60.3%) both significantly favouring the targeted arm. Patient-reported outcomes confirmed longer time to deterioration in global health status and physical functioning with sotorasib [59,60].

Subsequent cross-over in the control arm decoupled overall survival from the progression-free survival advantage, creating ongoing regulatory debate. Median overall survival was 10.6 months with sotorasib and 11.3 months with docetaxel (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.77-1.33, P=0.53) [59]. The lack of overall survival separation was primarily driven by crossover: 46 patients (26%) in the docetaxel arm crossed over to sotorasib per protocol upon confirmed disease progression, and an additional 13 patients received commercial G12C inhibitors, bringing the total effective crossover rate to 34% [59]. In parallel, protocol amendments requested by regulatory authorities reduced planned enrolment from 650 to 345 patients, leaving the trial underpowered to detect a survival difference in the presence of extensive post-progression crossover [59,60].

The phase III KRYSTAL-12 trial confirmed the PFS benefit of covalent G12C inhibition while demonstrating meaningful intracranial activity in patients with central nervous system metastases. The study enrolled 453 patients with pretreated *KRAS* G12C-mutated NSCLC, randomised 2:1 to adagrasib (600 mg twice daily) or docetaxel, without protocol-specified crossover [61]. At a median follow-up of 7.2 months, median PFS was 5.5 months with adagrasib versus 3.8 months with docetaxel (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.45-0.76, P<0.0001), with an ORR of 31.9% versus 9.2% (OR 4.68, P<0.0001) [61]. Among 114 patients with baseline brain metastases, intracranial ORR was 24% with adagrasib versus 11% with docetaxel, accompanied by a significant prolongation of intracranial PFS (HR 0.55), reflecting the central nervous system penetrance of adagrasib [61]. While early overall survival data showed a positive trend (HR 0.98), 44 docetaxel-arm patients (29%) crossed over to adagrasib off-protocol, obscuring the ultimate survival readout [61].

The phase III Krascendo 1 trial tested divarasib against an active sotorasib comparator, establishing the first statistically significant overall survival gain for a direct RAS inhibitor in NSCLC. The trial randomised 338 patients with pretreated advanced *KRAS* G12C-mutant NSCLC 1:1 to divarasib (400 mg once daily) or sotorasib (960 mg once daily) [62]. At a median follow-up of 14.3 months, median PFS reached 9.8 months with divarasib versus 5.7 months with sotorasib (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.43-0.78, P=0.0003), alongside an ORR of 58.4% versus 32.3% (P<0.0001) [62]. Importantly, because crossover between experimental arms was prohibited, median overall survival improved significantly to 17.7 months with divarasib compared with 12.0 months with sotorasib (HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.39-0.81, P=0.0019) [62].

Comparing these three randomised trials demonstrates that comparator selection and post-progression management govern whether overall survival differences emerge. When trials employ docetaxel controls and permit crossover (CodeBreaK 200, KRYSTAL-12), clear PFS gains are diluted by post-progression targeted therapy, complicating regulatory evaluation and reimbursement reviews [59,61]. Conversely, in Krascendo 1, where crossover was prohibited, higher target affinity (5- to 20-fold greater in vitro potency) translated into statistically significant improvements in both PFS and overall survival [62]. These findings indicate that post-progression access to active therapies drives the decoupling of PFS and overall survival in second-line trials, rather than a failure of target inhibition [60,63].

## 4.2 Metastatic colorectal cancer: vertical EGFR blockade and line-dependent requirements

Colorectal cancer displays pronounced intrinsic insensitivity to single-agent RAS inhibition, necessitating vertical combination strategies with anti-EGFR antibodies. Sotorasib or adagrasib monotherapy produces modest ORRs of 9.7% to 19% and median PFS of 4.0 to 5.6 months in pretreated metastatic colorectal cancer, falling well short of the 37% to 43% response rates documented in NSCLC [64–66]. Mechanistic work revealed that colorectal tumours express high levels of EGFR; inhibiting KRAS G12C relieves ERK-mediated feedback inhibition on EGFR, sparking rapid receptor phosphorylation that reactivates the MAPK cascade through wild-type NRAS and HRAS [67,68]. Overcoming this tissue-specific feedback loop requires adding an anti-EGFR antibody (panitumumab or cetuximab) to achieve vertical dual blockade (Figure 1a/Table 1) [66,68–70].

The phase III CodeBreaK 300 trial established the efficacy of vertical dual blockade in chemorefractory third-line colorectal cancer. The study randomised 160 patients with refractory *KRAS* G12C-mutant colorectal cancer 1:1:1 to sotorasib 960 mg plus panitumumab (n=53), sotorasib 240 mg plus panitumumab (n=53), or standard care (trifluridine-tipiracil or regorafenib, n=54) [69]. At a median follow-up of 13.6 months, the 960 mg sotorasib plus panitumumab group achieved a median PFS of 5.7 months versus 2.04 months with standard care (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.28-0.71, P=0.0004), alongside an ORR of 26.4% versus 0% (P<0.001) [69]. In updated analyses, median overall survival was 11.9 months in the 960 mg combination arm versus 10.3 months with standard care (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.41-1.18, P=0.20); while the trend favoured the targeted arm, statistical significance was not reached due to limited sample size and post-progression crossover [69,71].

The phase III KRYSTAL-10 trial evaluated whether targeted dual blockade could displace doublet chemotherapy in second-line colorectal cancer, but it failed to improve overall survival over standard cytotoxic regimens. The trial randomised 430 patients with *KRAS* G12C-mutated metastatic colorectal cancer whose disease progressed on frontline chemotherapy 1:1 to adagrasib plus cetuximab or investigator's choice chemotherapy (mFOLFOX6 or FOLFIRI with or without bevacizumab) [71,72]. At a median follow-up of 11.8 months, the targeted arm demonstrated a significant PFS prolongation (6.9 vs 5.6 months, HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.54-0.88, P=0.0028) and higher ORR (34.0% vs 18.6%, P<0.001) [72]. However, median overall survival was identical between arms (13.8 vs 14.2 months, HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.74-1.20, P=0.62) [72]. The durable control provided by cytotoxic doublets paired with anti-angiogenic agents, combined with a 42% crossover rate to G12C inhibitors in the chemotherapy arm, accounted for this survival convergence [72].

The discordant outcomes of CodeBreaK 300 and KRYSTAL-10 illustrate how comparator potency and treatment line constrain targeted replacement strategies. In third-line disease (CodeBreaK 300), standard salvage agents offer minimal activity (ORR <2%, median survival ~10 months), allowing targeted dual blockade to demonstrate clear PFS superiority and clinical utility. In second-line disease (KRYSTAL-10), cytotoxic doublets combined with bevacizumab establish an active comparator with a 14-month median survival that targeted doublets could not exceed. Chemotherapy-free targeted regimens cannot currently supplant standard second-line combination chemotherapy in colorectal cancer, positioning dual blockade primarily as a third-line salvage option or an adjunct to frontline backbones [69,71,72].

## 4.3 Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and NRAS-mutant melanoma: tri-complex glues vs downstream blockade

The phase III RASolute 302 trial demonstrated that active-state multi-RAS(ON) tri-complex inhibition doubles overall survival in pretreated pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Pancreatic tumours harbour *KRAS* mutations in roughly 90% of cases, dominated by G12D, G12V, and G12R, rendering covalent G12C inhibitors ineffective in this disease [14,73]. RASolute 302 randomised 500 patients with pretreated *KRAS*-mutant metastatic pancreatic cancer 1:1 to daraxonrasib (RMC-6236, 300 mg once daily) or investigator's choice chemotherapy (gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel or 5-fluorouracil plus liposomal irinotecan) [54]. At a median follow-up of 8.5 months, daraxonrasib extended median PFS to 7.2 months versus 3.6 months with chemotherapy (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.38-0.63, P<0.0001), with an ORR of 30.4% versus 10.8% (P<0.0001) [54]. Median overall survival doubled from 6.7 months with chemotherapy to 13.2 months with daraxonrasib (HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.30-0.53, P<0.0001), representing a 60% reduction in mortality risk that was consistent across G12D, G12V, and G12R subsets [54,74].

The pronounced overall survival benefit observed in pancreatic cancer stems from the rapid natural history of the disease and the absence of active third-line salvage therapies. Pretreated metastatic pancreatic cancer exhibits aggressive progression, with chemotherapy control groups showing a median survival of only 6.7 months and few established subsequent treatment options. Without effective salvage regimens to rescue control-arm patients after progression, the PFS prolongation provided by daraxonrasib translated directly into a survival gain, unhindered by crossover dilution [54].

The phase III NEMO trial assessed downstream MEK inhibition in advanced *NRAS*-mutant melanoma, confirming a PFS prolongation that failed to confer an overall survival benefit. The trial randomised 402 patients with pretreated or treatment-naive advanced *NRAS*-mutant melanoma 2:1 to binimetinib (45 mg twice daily) or dacarbazine chemotherapy [75]. Binimetinib extended median PFS to 2.8 months versus 1.5 months with dacarbazine (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.47-0.80, P<0.001) and improved ORR (15% vs 7%, P=0.015) [75]. However, median overall survival was equivalent between arms (11.0 vs 10.1 months, HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.75-1.33, P=0.999) [75]. The lack of survival benefit was driven by subsequent immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4), which rescued post-progression survival across both study groups [75].

Contrasting RASolute 302 and NEMO underscores how post-progression therapeutic options shape overall survival outcomes. In pancreatic cancer, where effective salvage therapies are lacking, PFS gains translated directly into overall survival extension [54]. In melanoma, by contrast, effective post-progression immunotherapies levelled out the survival curves, erasing the transient advantage gained with MEK inhibition [75]. Evaluating the true survival impact of RAS-directed agents therefore requires accounting for disease trajectory, crossover frequency, and the availability of downstream salvage lines.

## 4.4 Cross-trial safety architecture, tolerability, and chronic cumulative burdens

Cross-trial safety assessments must distinguish between treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) and all-cause treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs). When evaluating grade >= 3 toxicities, CodeBreaK 200 (33.1% vs 40.4%), KRYSTAL-12 (47.0% vs 45.7%), and Krascendo 1 (24.6% vs 34.5%) reported TRAEs, demonstrating rates comparable to or lower than docetaxel chemotherapy [59,61,62]. Similarly, KRYSTAL-10 (41.2% vs 54.5%) and RASolute 302 (61.8% vs 69.6%) documented lower high-grade adverse event rates than chemotherapy comparators [54,71]. Conversely, early trials such as NEMO reported grade >= 3 all-cause TEAEs of 68% (vs 46% with dacarbazine) [75]. Conflating these reporting methodologies can distort perceptions of clinical tolerability [61,76].

Toxicity profiles mirror the biochemical mechanism of each agent and its respective combination partners. First-generation covalent G12C monotherapies present predominantly with gastrointestinal and hepatic toxicities: sotorasib and adagrasib trigger low-grade diarrhoea (32%-48%), nausea, and transaminase elevations (10%-15%), with grade >= 3 ALT/AST increases occurring in 6%-8% without myelosuppression [42,59]. Adding anti-EGFR antibodies introduces characteristic EGFR-related epithelial toxicities, primarily acneiform rash (11%), paronychia (6%), and hypomagnesaemia (6%) [68,69]. The tri-complex molecular glue daraxonrasib reflects partial inhibition of wild-type RAS(ON), producing superficial rash (86.3%, grade 3: 4.2%) and stomatitis (54.8%), while sparing bone marrow reserves (grade 3 neutropenia <2% vs 24% with chemotherapy) [54]. By contrast, MEK inhibition with binimetinib causes broad systemic toxicities, including creatine kinase elevations (19%), serous retinal detachment (12%), peripheral oedema (36%), and hypertension (7%), leading to dose reductions in 61% and discontinuations in 25% [75–78].

Clinical toxicity management is shifting from mitigating acute high-grade events to managing chronic, cumulative low-grade burdens. While transaminase elevations from covalent inhibitors typically manifest within the first three months and resolve with temporary dose pauses or corticosteroids, persistent grade 1-2 diarrhoea, fatigue, anorexia, and mucosal dryness often endure for months. These low-grade toxicities gradually erode patient quality of life and provoke dose modifications or treatment discontinuation. In the adjuvant setting, sustaining long-term treatment adherence will represent a critical determinant of therapeutic success [76–78].

# 5. Translational hurdles, emerging paradigms, and frontline migration

## 5.1 First-line combination strategies and adjuvant escalation

Clinical trials are advancing direct RAS inhibitors into earlier disease stages and frontline combination settings. Of the 1,182 RAS-focused trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, roughly 30% evaluate frontline or early-stage interventions, with combination studies outnumbering monotherapy trials by more than three to one (Figure 4) [79]. In frontline NSCLC, three major phase III trials are underway: CodeBreaK 202 (sotorasib plus chemotherapy, N=750), Krascendo 2 (divarasib plus chemotherapy, N=600), and SUNRAY-01 (adagrasib plus chemotherapy or chemo-immunotherapy, N=1,264), each powered for PFS and overall survival endpoints [80–82].

Replacing chemo-immunotherapy with targeted monotherapy in treatment-naive cohorts faces pharmacodynamic and immunological hurdles. Immune checkpoint inhibitors can generate durable, antigen-specific memory T-cell responses that support long-term disease control in a subset of patients, whereas small-molecule RAS inhibitors induce transient cytostasis that often rebounds upon drug cessation. Direct single-agent RAS inhibition is unlikely to surpass frontline chemo-immunotherapy across unselected populations. Instead, rational strategies aim to use targeted agents to rapidly reduce tumour burden and reshape the immune microenvironment, combining them with immune checkpoint blockade or chemotherapy doublets rather than pursuing monotherapy replacement [55,81,83,84].

Frontline multi-agent combinations are also advancing in gastrointestinal malignancies. In frontline metastatic pancreatic cancer, the phase III RASolute 303 trial (N=900) compares daraxonrasib monotherapy and daraxonrasib plus gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel against standard chemotherapy, while RASolute 309 (N=400) assesses daraxonrasib maintenance [81,85]. In colorectal cancer, CodeBreaK 301 (N=450) evaluates sotorasib plus panitumumab against mFOLFOX6 plus bevacizumab in the first-line setting, testing the targeted regimen against standard frontline chemotherapy [81].

Adjuvant trials have opened in completely resected NSCLC and pancreatic cancer, requiring rigorous control arms reflecting established standards of care. SUNRAY-02 (N=700) and Krascendo 3 (N=400) evaluate adjuvant G12C inhibition following surgery and standard adjuvant chemotherapy in resected stage II-IIIB NSCLC, incorporating adjuvant immune checkpoint blockade into comparator arms [79,81]. These trials rely on disease-free survival as primary regulatory endpoints, with overall survival serving as the ultimate benchmark of curative benefit [81,85].

## 5.2 Mechanisms of acquired resistance and multimodal counterstrategies

Sustained selective pressure from direct RAS inhibitors invariably triggers heterogeneous acquired resistance mechanisms (Figure 5a) [86,87]. These alterations fall into three broad classes: on-target secondary mutations, bypass pathway reactivation, and histological transformation. On-target modifications include switch-II pocket mutations (Y96D/C, R68S, H95D/Q/R) that impair drug binding, dynamic switch mutations (G12D/V/R, G13D, Q61H/K/L), and high-level *KRAS* gene amplification [87,88]. Bypass resistance involves receptor tyrosine kinase amplification (*EGFR*, *HER2*, *MET*), downstream mutations in MAPK or PI3K effectors (*BRAF* V600E, *PIK3CA*, *MAP2K1*), or oncogenic gene fusions (*RET*, *ALK*, *FGFR3*) [87,89]. Histological transformation encompasses epithelial-mesenchymal transition and neuroendocrine small-cell transdifferentiation, frequently accompanied by functional loss of *TP53* and *RB1* [90,91]. Paired tissue and ctDNA analyses detect resistance mechanisms in 45% of NSCLC cases (17/38) on monotherapy and 74% of colorectal cancer cases (25/34) on dual therapy, predominantly as polyclonal mixtures (Figure 5b) [87,88]. Countering these mechanisms requires tailored combinations: switching to tri-complex molecular glues or degraders for on-target pocket mutations, co-targeting SHP2, MET, or EGFR for bypass activations, and re-escalating cytotoxic chemotherapy for histological transformation [55,91,92].

## 5.3 Biomarkers and patient stratification beyond binary mutation testing

Because objective response rates across advanced solid tumours remain under 50%, baseline molecular profiling must extend beyond binary mutation calls to multi-gene and transcriptomic co-determinants. Concomitant *KEAP1* mutations consistently associate with abbreviated PFS (<3.0–4.1 months) and attenuated overall survival across cohorts treated with sotorasib, adagrasib, or divarasib (e.g., median OS 5.4 vs 19.0 months, HR 3.6 in KRYSTAL-1), establishing *KEAP1* as a reproducible pan-inhibitor marker of primary resistance [93–95]. Intriguingly, the clinical impact of *STK11* co-mutations exhibits a striking paradox between targeted inhibition and immunotherapy: while *STK11* loss produces near-complete refractoriness to immune checkpoint blockade, *STK11*-mutant tumours treated with direct KRAS G12C inhibitors achieve objective response rates comparable to wild-type cohorts (45% vs 45% in KRYSTAL-1, ~40% in CodeBreaK 100), demonstrating that primary immune evasion does not preclude direct oncogene targeting [94,95]. However, deconvolution analyses from the KRYSTAL-1 trial demonstrate that the adverse survival impact of *STK11* is largely mediated by concurrent *KEAP1* alterations or elevated NRF2 transcriptional signatures [95]. In fact, 50% of *KEAP1*-wild-type/*STK11*-mutant tumours exhibit high NRF2 activation, promoting metabolic resilience, cellular plasticity, and adeno-to-squamous reprogramming that attenuates long-term dependency on RAS signaling [95]. Remarkably, the triple-negative subset (*KEAP1*-WT / *STK11*-WT / NRF2-low), representing 32% of patients, achieved an extraordinary median PFS of 16.9 months and an unreached median overall survival upon adagrasib therapy [95]. This multi-omic signature defines an exceptional-responder population and underscores that co-mutation and transcriptional profiling should guide prospective patient stratification.

Beyond co-mutations, genomic dosage effects and baseline clinical characteristics exert decisive influence on therapeutic response. *KRAS* copy number gains, focal amplification, and high baseline mutant allele frequency (VAF) correlate with shortened response durability, reflecting an elevated baseline flux of active GTP-bound GTPase that outpaces stoichiometric drug engagement and accelerates the selection of subclonal resistant variants [87,88,95]. Among clinical presentation features, baseline central nervous system (CNS) metastases—present in 30%–40% of advanced *KRAS* G12C NSCLC—represent a critical clinical decision node. Compounds differ substantially in blood-brain barrier penetration: adagrasib exhibits sustained cerebrospinal fluid accumulation and potent intracranial target engagement; in the prospective KRYSTAL-1 CNS cohort and the confirmatory phase III KRYSTAL-12 trial, adagrasib established superior intracranial efficacy over cytotoxic chemotherapy (intracranial ORR 24%–42% vs 11%; CNS PFS HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.31–0.98), providing a compelling clinical biomarker for selecting between competing covalent agents [61,96]. Histological subtype and organ tropism further dictate responsiveness: patients with invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (IMA) histology exhibit markedly reduced response rates to G12C inhibitors (ORR 14% vs 41%, P=0.01), driven by alternative dependency on lineage-specifying transcription factors such as HNF4A [22]. In metastatic colorectal cancer, primary tumour sidedness (right vs left colon) similarly modulates response depth, reflecting baseline differences in MAPK feedback kinetics and EGFR receptor density [15,16,69].

In the blood, dynamic circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) tracking provides powerful pharmacodynamic stratification and early response assessment. Baseline ctDNA shedding mirrors visceral disease burden, while rapid clearance of mutant *KRAS* copies by Cycle 2, Day 1 (week 3–4) strongly predicts prolonged PFS and extended overall survival (objective response rate 80% in clearers vs 8% in non-clearers), whereas persisting ctDNA shedding identifies patients requiring early treatment intensification [61,97,98]. Ultra-sensitive longitudinal ctDNA sequencing detects polyclonal on-target pocket mutations (*KRAS* Y96D, A59S) and bypass pathway alterations (*BRAF*, *MET*) months before radiographic progression, providing a molecular lead-time to preemptive combination switching [87,98,99]. Finally, treatment sequencing between immunotherapy and direct RAS inhibitors entails critical clinical safety ramifications. Administering sotorasib immediately following anti-PD-(L)1 immune checkpoint inhibitors triggers severe grade 3–4 autoimmune-like hepatotoxicity (ALT/AST elevations >5–20× upper limit of normal) in up to 35%–50% of patients, driven by prolonged antibody half-lives and compound-mediated immune reactivation [76,77]. This hazard mandates an observational washout period of at least 30 to 90 days following checkpoint blockade, or the preferential deployment of compounds with lower immunological hepatotoxicity profiles (such as adagrasib or divarasib) [61,62,77]. Prospective randomized trials embedding ctDNA-directed treatment escalation and multi-omic stratification remain an urgent unmet clinical need [94,99].

# 6. Conclusions and future perspectives

Direct pharmacological inhibition of RAS has validated decades of structural biology by establishing reproducible tumour regressions and progression-free survival gains in advanced NSCLC, colorectal cancer, and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. In pancreatic cancer, active-state tri-complex inhibition has achieved an unprecedented doubling of overall survival in the second-line setting. Concurrently, the randomised trial evidence delineates key clinical boundaries: when control arms offer effective post-progression options and permit crossover, overall survival endpoints are diluted; in settings lacking active salvage lines, PFS benefits translate cleanly into survival gains. Looking forward, single-agent therapy will increasingly shift toward salvage roles. The ultimate success of RAS precision oncology rests on three imperatives: deploying vertical and horizontal combinations to intercept adaptive feedback and bypass pathways; leveraging pan-RAS(ON) and allele-selective molecular glues to overcome non-G12C variants and on-target secondary mutations; and advancing through rigorous randomised trials into frontline combinations and early-stage adjuvant settings to convert disease control into curative benefit.
