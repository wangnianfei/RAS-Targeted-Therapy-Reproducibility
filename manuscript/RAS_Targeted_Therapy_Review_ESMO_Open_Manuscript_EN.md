**Highlights**

- Direct RAS inhibitors now target both inactive (OFF) and active (ON) RAS.

**Abstract**

**Background:** Direct inhibition of RAS oncoproteins, long considered intractable, has been achieved with covalent inhibitors that bind the switch-II pocket of inactive KRAS G12C and with tri-complex inhibitors that bind active RAS together with cyclophilin A.

**Methods & Evidence Synthesis:** We review the structural basis of these drug classes and the results of seven phase III randomised trials in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), colorectal cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and melanoma. We also analyse 1,151 RAS-related interventional oncology trials in ClinicalTrials.gov, 353 of which test a directly RAS-targeted agent in solid tumours.

**Key Findings:** RAS-pathway inhibitors improved the objective response rate in all seven trials and progression-free survival (PFS) in six. Overall survival (OS) was improved by divarasib over first-generation KRAS G12C inhibitors in pretreated NSCLC and by the multi-RAS(ON) inhibitor daraxonrasib over chemotherapy in pancreatic cancer (median 13.2 versus 6.7 months). Sotorasib did not improve OS over docetaxel, a comparison complicated by crossover, and OS data for adagrasib remain immature. In colorectal cancer, KRAS G12C inhibitors need EGFR co-blockade: sotorasib plus panitumumab improved PFS in refractory disease, whereas adagrasib plus cetuximab did not improve PFS or OS over second-line chemotherapy, and its US accelerated approval was withdrawn at the sponsor's request. Acquired resistance involves secondary *KRAS* alterations, receptor tyrosine kinase and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway bypass and histological transformation, and *STK11* and *KEAP1* co-mutations are associated with poorer outcomes.

**Conclusions:** Priorities include inhibitors for non-G12C alleles, first-line and perioperative trials, combinations matched to resistance mechanisms, and trial designs that pre-specify how crossover will be handled in the analysis of OS.

**1. Introduction: direct RAS targeting in solid tumours**

Activating mutations in *RAS* genes are among the most common oncogenic drivers in human cancer: about 19% of patients with cancer carry a *RAS* mutation, corresponding to roughly 3.4 million new cases a year worldwide \[1\]. For nearly four decades after *HRAS*, *KRAS* and *NRAS* were identified as transforming oncogenes in the early 1980s \[2–4\], RAS proteins were considered undruggable.

This changed in 2013, when Ostrem and colleagues described an inducible pocket beneath switch II (the switch-II pocket, S-IIP) in GDP-bound KRAS(G12C) \[5\]. Covalent inhibitors that bind this pocket trap KRAS(G12C) in its inactive state without affecting wild-type RAS \[5,6\]. Accelerated approvals of sotorasib in 2021 and adagrasib in 2022 for previously treated *KRAS* G12C-mutant advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) showed that RAS can be inhibited directly in patients \[7–9\]. The field has since expanded to second-generation covalent G12C inhibitors (divarasib, olomorasib, glecirasib), non-covalent inhibitors of other alleles such as KRAS G12D (MRTX1133), and tri-complex inhibitors of active RAS that act together with cyclophilin A (daraxonrasib) \[10–14\], as well as allosteric inhibitors of the exchange factor SOS1 \[15,16\].

After summarising RAS biology, allele epidemiology and drug mechanisms, we compare the phase III evidence and review registered trials, acquired resistance and future directions, distinguishing what randomised trials have shown from what remains to be confirmed.

**2. Biology and epidemiology of the RAS pathway in solid tumours**

**2.1 The binary GTPase cycle and structural basis of activation**

RAS proteins are binary molecular switches regulated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) and GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs). The three human *RAS* genes (*KRAS*, *NRAS* and *HRAS*) encode conserved 21-kDa small GTPases \[2\], each with a catalytic G-domain (residues 1–166) and a C-terminal hypervariable region that undergoes CAAX-directed farnesylation and carboxymethylation for membrane anchoring \[2,4\]. Two loops of the G-domain, switch I (residues 30–38) and switch II (residues 60–76), govern nucleotide binding and effector engagement (Figure 1a) \[3,17\]. SOS1 and SOS2 catalyse the release of GDP, allowing abundant cellular GTP to bind and inducing a closed conformation that binds effectors \[2,18,19\]. GAPs such as NF1 accelerate intrinsic GTP hydrolysis about 10<sup>5</sup>-fold by inserting an 'arginine finger' into the catalytic site, restoring the inactive GDP-bound state \[20\]. Oncogenic missense mutations at codons 12, 13 or 61 impair GAP-stimulated GTP hydrolysis, so that the mutant protein accumulates in its active, GTP-bound state \[20\].

Active RAS signals mainly through the RAF–MEK–ERK (MAPK) and PI3K–AKT–mTOR cascades \[2,3,21\]. ERK phosphorylates upstream receptor tyrosine kinases, SOS1 and CRAF, creating negative feedback that attenuates pathway flux \[21,22\]; inhibiting RAS or its effectors relieves this feedback and reactivates receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) such as EGFR, MET and HER2, which promotes resistance (Section 5.2) \[22,23\].

**2.2 RAS mutation frequency and allele distribution across solid tumours**

*RAS* mutation frequencies depend on the cohort. In the TCGA PanCancer Atlas, 10.7% of 10,967 samples from 32 studies of largely untreated primary tumours carry a *KRAS*, *NRAS* or *HRAS* mutation (cBioPortal query of the PanCancer Atlas data \[25\]). Estimates that pool mutation databases are higher, at about 19% to 25% of cancers \[1,26\], and in the MSK-IMPACT clinical sequencing cohort of 10,945 samples from patients with advanced cancer, 17.8% carry a *RAS* missense mutation \[27\]; such estimates differ mainly because of the tumour types each dataset contains \[1\]. *KRAS* accounts for approximately 75% of *RAS*-mutant cancers, *NRAS* for 17% and *HRAS* for 7% \[1\].

*KRAS* mutations predominate in adenocarcinomas of the pancreas (about 90%), colorectum (45% to 50%) and lung (30% to 35%) \[1,26\] (MSK-IMPACT: 90.4%, 43.1% and 26.9%, the last for all NSCLC; Figure 2a) \[27\]. *NRAS* mutations occur mainly in cutaneous melanoma (about 17%; 21.4% of melanomas in MSK-IMPACT), acute myeloid leukaemia (about 14%) and thyroid carcinoma \[1,26\], and *HRAS* mutations, less frequent overall (1%–2%), are enriched in head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, bladder urothelial cancers and salivary gland tumours \[1,28\].

Allele spectra differ across tumour types (Figure 2b). In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, *KRAS* G12D accounts for about 41% of *KRAS* mutations, followed by G12V (32% to 33%) and G12R (13% to 16%), whereas G12C is rare (about 2%) \[27,29,30\]. In metastatic colorectal cancer, *KRAS* exon 2 mutations occur in 37.8% of patients, and extended *RAS* and atypical *RAS* mutations in a further 9.5% and 1.2% \[31\]; G12C accounts for about 7% of *KRAS* missense mutations (about 3% of all colorectal cancers) \[27\]. In NSCLC, G-to-T transversions caused by tobacco carcinogens make G12C the most common variant \[1\], accounting for about 30% to 43% of *KRAS* mutations in Western and Chinese cohorts, followed by G12V and G12D (each about 13% to 19%) \[27,32–34\]. In cutaneous melanoma, *NRAS* accounts for most *RAS* mutations \[1\].

Co-occurring alterations add heterogeneity. In *KRAS*-mutant NSCLC, co-mutations in *TP53* (about 40%), *STK11* (about 30%) and *KEAP1* (20%–25%) define distinct subgroups \[35,36\]. Tumours with co-mutated *KRAS* and *TP53* (KP) tend to have a high tumour mutational burden, immune infiltration and sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors \[35,37\], whereas tumours with co-mutated *KRAS* and *STK11* (KL) show STING silencing, an immune-cold microenvironment and primary resistance to anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy \[36,38,39\]. *KEAP1* loss, which often co-occurs with *STK11* loss, activates NRF2 and is associated with shorter benefit from KRAS G12C inhibitors \[40,41\].

**3. Biophysical barriers and direct pharmacological targeting strategies**

**3.1 Discovery of the switch-II pocket**

Three biophysical obstacles hindered direct targeting of RAS. First, RAS binds GDP and GTP with picomolar affinity, whereas cellular GDP and GTP concentrations are in the millimolar range, which ruled out the active-site competition used by kinase inhibitors against micromolar-affinity ATP \[3,42\]. Second, the G-domain surface is smooth and lacks deep pockets \[3,42\]. Third, the active and inactive states differ mainly in the two switch regions, so compounds that bind the inactive state must compete with exchange factors that rapidly reload GTP \[6\].

Indirect strategies failed. Farnesyltransferase inhibitors such as tipifarnib were evaded because KRAS and NRAS can be prenylated by geranylgeranyltransferase-I instead \[3,43\], and MEK inhibitors, given alone or with docetaxel, did not improve overall survival in *KRAS*-mutant NSCLC, in part because of feedback reactivation of the pathway \[21,44–46\].

Crystal structures of GDP-bound KRAS(G12C) solved by Ostrem and Shokat revealed the S-IIP, a shallow cavity between switch II and the central β-sheet that is absent in GTP-bound RAS (Figure 1a) \[5\]. Ligands in this pocket lock both switches in an inactive conformation, favour the GDP-bound state and abolish effector binding \[5,47\]. Because KRAS(G12C) retains intrinsic GTPase activity close to that of wild-type KRAS, whereas mutants such as G12V retain much less, KRAS(G12C) cycles through the GDP-bound state, where it can be covalently trapped \[6,48\].

**3.2 Four contemporary mechanistic classes of direct RAS inhibitors**

The first class comprises covalent inhibitors of GDP-bound (OFF-state) KRAS(G12C) (Figure 1a; Supplementary Table S1). Sotorasib and adagrasib both bind the switch-II pocket and form a covalent adduct with Cys12, but they engage the pocket differently, which shapes their resistance profiles (Section 5.2) \[49,50\]. Adagrasib also penetrates the central nervous system (CNS) and has intracranial activity in patients with untreated brain metastases \[51\].

Second-generation G12C inhibitors were designed for greater potency, selectivity and tissue distribution. Divarasib is 5 to 20 times as potent and up to 50 times as selective in vitro as sotorasib and adagrasib, and suppresses ERK phosphorylation at lower concentrations \[10,52\]; olomorasib produced intracranial responses in patients with untreated, active brain metastases in its first-in-human study \[53\].

The second class, non-covalent inhibitors, extends direct inhibition to alleles that lack a reactive cysteine. The aspartate of KRAS G12D cannot be tethered by acrylamide chemistry; MRTX1133 instead occupies the switch-II pocket through ionic and hydrogen-bonding interactions with Asp12 and surrounding residues, binds GDP-bound KRAS(G12D) with sub-nanomolar affinity and was efficacious in a *KRAS* G12D-mutant xenograft model \[12\]. Other non-covalent compounds, such as BI-2852, bind a pocket between switch I and switch II and are not allele-selective \[54\].

The third class comprises tri-complex inhibitors of GTP-bound (ON-state) RAS (Figure 1b). These molecules bind cyclophilin A (CYPA), and the drug–CYPA complex binds RAS next to switch I and switch II, blocking effector binding without displacing GTP \[13,55\]. Daraxonrasib (RMC-6236) and its preclinical analogue RMC-7977 inhibit multiple active RAS isoforms, diverse codon-12 and codon-13 variants and wild-type RAS \[13,56\]. Mutant-selective tri-complex inhibitors also form a covalent bond with the mutant residue: elironrasib (RMC-6291) with Cys12 of KRAS G12C \[55\] and zoldonrasib (RMC-9805) with Asp12 of KRAS G12D \[57\]. Because multi-selective inhibitors also suppress wild-type RAS in healthy tissues, their therapeutic window depends on dose selection \[56\].

The fourth class comprises targeted degraders, which recruit an E3 ubiquitin ligase to KRAS; the pan-KRAS degrader ACBI3 suppressed pathway signalling more durably than inhibition in preclinical models \[58\]. Allosteric SOS1 inhibitors (such as BI 1701963 and MRTX0902) block SOS1-mediated nucleotide exchange but are not direct RAS inhibitors, and like SHP2 inhibitors they are being developed mainly as combination partners (Section 5.3) \[15,16\].

**4.2 Second-line monotherapy in non-small cell lung cancer: CodeBreaK 200, KRYSTAL-12, and Krascendo 1**

In *KRAS* G12C-mutant NSCLC progressing after platinum chemotherapy and anti-PD-(L)1 therapy, two phase III trials compared G12C inhibitor monotherapy with docetaxel (Table 1, Figure 3). In CodeBreaK 200, 345 patients were randomised to sotorasib or docetaxel; sotorasib improved progression-free survival (PFS) by blinded independent central review (BICR) (median 5.6 versus 4.5 months; hazard ratio \[HR\] 0.66, 95% confidence interval \[CI\] 0.51–0.86) and the objective response rate (ORR; 28.1% versus 13.2%) \[60\].

Overall survival (OS) did not differ (median 10.6 versus 11.3 months; HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.77–1.33; Figure 3) \[60\]. Crossover probably contributed: 26% of patients in the docetaxel arm crossed over to sotorasib after progression, and 34% received a KRAS G12C inhibitor at some point \[60,66\]. The open-label design was a further limitation: 13% of patients assigned to docetaxel (23 of 174) were never treated, compared with 1% (2 of 171) assigned to sotorasib \[60\]. Together with a median PFS gain of about five weeks, these issues led the FDA Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee in October 2023 to question whether the PFS result could be reliably interpreted \[66\]; full approval was not granted (Supplementary Table S2).

In KRYSTAL-12, adagrasib also improved PFS over docetaxel in 453 patients (median 5.5 versus 3.8 months; HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.45–0.76) and increased the ORR (31.9% versus 9.2%) \[61\]. OS data were immature at the primary report \[61\], and no final analysis had been published by our cut-off date; after an FDA-requested protocol amendment, about 29% of patients in the docetaxel arm crossed over to adagrasib \[67\], which may attenuate any survival difference. The intracranial activity of adagrasib is relevant to the management of brain metastases (Section 6.1) \[51,69\].

Krascendo 1 provided the first head-to-head comparison between direct RAS inhibitors: 338 patients with previously treated *KRAS* G12C-mutant advanced NSCLC were randomised to divarasib or to investigator's choice of sotorasib (156 control patients) or adagrasib (15) (Table 1, Figure 3) \[62\]. Divarasib improved PFS by BICR (median 9.8 versus 5.7 months; HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.76) and the confirmed ORR (58.4% versus 32.3%) and, at the first interim analysis, OS (median 17.7 versus 12.0 months; HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.41–0.78), with fewer grade 3 or 4 treatment-related adverse events (24.6% versus 34.5%) \[62\]. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that deeper and more sustained target inhibition improves outcomes, although the trial was not designed to test this mechanism. Because the control arm received an active KRAS G12C inhibitor rather than docetaxel, the survival advantage cannot be extrapolated to a comparison with chemotherapy.

**4.3 Colorectal cancer and the biological requirement for vertical EGFR co-inhibition**

KRAS G12C inhibitor monotherapy has limited activity in metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), with response rates of about 10% to 20% and median PFS of 4 to 6 months \[70,71\]. This reflects EGFR-dependent feedback \[23\]: inhibition of KRAS(G12C) relieves negative feedback on EGFR, SOS1 and CRAF, and EGFR signalling then reactivates wild-type and mutant RAS (Section 5.2) \[23\]. Adding an anti-EGFR antibody (cetuximab or panitumumab) is intended to block both the driver and this feedback \[63,71,72\].

In CodeBreaK 300, 160 patients with chemorefractory *KRAS* G12C-mutant mCRC were randomised to sotorasib 960 mg or 240 mg plus panitumumab or to investigator's choice of trifluridine–tipiracil or regorafenib (Table 1, Figure 3) \[63\]. Median PFS was 5.6 months with 960 mg (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.30–0.80) and 3.9 months with 240 mg (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.36–0.93), versus 2.2 months with standard therapy, and the confirmed ORR was 26.4%, 5.7% and 0%, respectively \[63\].

KRYSTAL-10 tested whether targeted dual blockade could replace second-line chemotherapy doublets. In 461 patients with *KRAS* G12C-mutant mCRC, adagrasib plus cetuximab roughly tripled the ORR compared with FOLFIRI or mFOLFOX6 with or without a VEGF/VEGFR inhibitor (47% versus 16%) but met neither dual primary endpoint. Median PFS by BICR was 7.5 versus 8.1 months (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.71–1.13) and median OS 21.6 versus 21.7 months (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.67–1.03) (Table 1, Figure 3) \[64\]. Following this result, the accelerated-approval indication for adagrasib plus cetuximab in previously treated *KRAS* G12C-mutant mCRC was withdrawn at the sponsor's request, as recorded by the US FDA on 1 September 2026 \[73\] (Supplementary Table S2).

**4.4 Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma: breakthrough with multi-RAS(ON) inhibition**

RASolute 302 tested multi-RAS(ON) inhibition in metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, in which more than 90% of tumours harbour oncogenic *KRAS* alterations (predominantly G12D, G12V and G12R) that G12C inhibitors cannot target (Table 1, Figure 3) \[59,74,75\]. It randomised 500 previously treated patients to daraxonrasib (300 mg once daily) or investigator's choice of chemotherapy, without crossover; the dual primary endpoints were OS and PFS in patients with *RAS* G12 mutations (459 patients, 91.8%) \[59\]. At the first interim analysis, daraxonrasib prolonged OS in this population (median 13.2 versus 6.6 months; HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.30–0.54) and in the overall population (median 13.2 versus 6.7 months; HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.30–0.53; Figure 3). It also approximately doubled PFS (overall population: median 7.2 versus 3.6 months; HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.38–0.64; Figure 3) and increased the ORR (31.6% versus 11.2%) \[59\]. Treatment-related adverse events led to discontinuation in 1.2% versus 11.2% of patients \[59\]. The absence of crossover and the scarcity of effective later-line options probably helped the PFS benefit translate into an OS benefit; of note, 84.9% of patients assigned to chemotherapy received any trial treatment, compared with 97.2% of those assigned to daraxonrasib \[59\].

**4.5 Cutaneous melanoma and *NRAS*-mutant disease: lessons from the NEMO trial**

In the phase III NEMO trial, the MEK inhibitor binimetinib was compared with dacarbazine in 402 patients with advanced *NRAS*-mutant cutaneous melanoma (Table 1, Figure 3) \[65\]. Binimetinib improved PFS (median 2.8 versus 1.5 months; HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.47–0.80) and the ORR (15% versus 7%) but not OS (median 11.0 versus 10.1 months; HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.75–1.33) \[65\]. Crossover was not permitted, but subsequent immunotherapy in both arms may have diluted any survival difference. The modest PFS gain of 1.3 months is consistent with the limited durability of MEK inhibitor monotherapy (Section 3.1).

**4.6 Safety profiles and toxicity management across drug classes**

Cross-trial comparisons of safety must distinguish treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) from all-cause treatment-emergent adverse events and account for differences in treatment duration. In the two trials against docetaxel, grade 3 or higher TRAEs occurred in 33.1% versus 40.4% of patients with sotorasib \[60\] and in 47.0% versus 45.7% with adagrasib \[61\]. In a pooled analysis of 549 patients treated with sotorasib monotherapy, grade 3 or higher TRAEs occurred in 26.8%; diarrhoea, nausea and transaminase elevations were the most common, and most resolved with dose interruption or reduction \[76\]. Gastrointestinal and hepatic events also predominate with adagrasib \[9,77\].

Combinations change the toxicity profile. In CodeBreaK 300, grade 3 or higher TRAEs occurred in 35.8% of patients receiving sotorasib 960 mg plus panitumumab and in 43.1% of those receiving standard therapy, with skin toxicity and hypomagnesaemia most common with the combination \[63\]. Combining or sequencing covalent G12C inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors raises the risk of hepatotoxicity. In a French multicentre cohort, severe sotorasib-related hepatotoxicity occurred in 33% of patients whose last treatment before sotorasib was anti-PD-(L)1 therapy, compared with 11% of other patients \[78\]. In a pooled analysis, treatment-related hepatotoxicity occurred in 33.3% of patients who stopped immunotherapy less than 3 months before sotorasib and in 13.8% of those who stopped it earlier \[76\]. With olomorasib plus pembrolizumab, grade 3 or higher TRAEs occurred in 33.3% of patients and led to discontinuation of both drugs in 6.5% \[79\].

**5. Registered trials, acquired resistance and combination strategies**

**5.1 Registered clinical trials of directly RAS-targeted agents**

Of 1,151 RAS-related interventional oncology trials registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (search cut-off date: 18 November 2026), 353 test a directly RAS-targeted agent in patients with solid tumours. Trials of agents acting upstream or downstream of RAS (such as SHP2, SOS1, RAF or MEK inhibitors), trials that only select patients by *RAS* status and trials in haematological malignancies are not counted; the search and the record-level review are described in the Supplementary Methods. Of these 353 trials, 43 (12.2%) are phase 2/3 or 3, and 319 (90.4%) were first posted in 2021 or later. KRAS G12C inhibitors are the most common drug class (201 trials), followed by KRAS G12D inhibitors (60), RAS vaccines, T-cell receptor therapies or T-cell engagers (40) and multi-RAS(ON) or pan-RAS inhibitors (39). The United States hosts at least one site in 198 trials (56.1%) and mainland China in 152 (43.1%), and industry is the lead sponsor of 241 trials (68.3%), including all 43 phase 2/3 or 3 trials.

Regulatory pathways have diverged (Supplementary Table S2). Sotorasib and adagrasib received accelerated approval from the US FDA and conditional marketing authorisation from the European Medicines Agency; after the October 2023 advisory committee meeting, the FDA did not grant sotorasib full approval, whereas its European conditional marketing authorisation was renewed in November 2024 with CodeBreaK 200 results added to the product information \[80\]. In China, the National Medical Products Administration granted conditional approval to garsorasib, fulzerasib and glecirasib, each supported by a single-arm phase II trial in previously treated NSCLC \[11,81,82\].

**5.2 Mechanisms of acquired resistance and multimodal counterstrategies**

Acquired resistance limits the duration of direct RAS inhibition, with median response durations in monotherapy trials rarely exceeding 6 to 11 months \[8,9,60,61\]. Analyses of paired baseline and progression samples show on-target *KRAS* alterations, reactivation of bypass pathways and changes in histology (Figure 4; Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Figure S1) \[50,83–86\]. Among 38 patients with acquired resistance to adagrasib, switch-II pocket mutations (R68S, H95D/Q/R and Y96C) were found in 4 (11%) and an acquired *KRAS* mutation or amplification in 9 \[50\]; among 43 patients treated with sotorasib, treatment-emergent alterations, including in *KRAS*, *NRAS*, *BRAF*, *EGFR* and *FGFR2*, were found in 27 \[84\]. Because His95 interacts directly with adagrasib but probably only weakly with sotorasib, H95 mutations conferred resistance to adagrasib but not to sotorasib in vitro, whereas Y96C and R68S conferred resistance to both drugs, and mutations outside the pocket that enhance nucleotide exchange conferred more modest resistance \[50\]. However, each clinical H95 mutation co-occurred with other alterations expected to confer resistance to both drugs, so this asymmetry does not by itself support switching from adagrasib to sotorasib \[50\].

Off-target mechanisms restore signalling through parallel or downstream pathways (Figure 4) \[50,83,84\]. After adagrasib, they included *MET* amplification, the only identified mechanism in 2 of 38 patients, oncogenic fusions involving *ALK*, *RET*, *BRAF*, *RAF1* and *FGFR3*, and acquired *BRAF* V600E, *NRAS* Q61K and *MAP2K1* mutations that reactivate ERK signalling independently of KRAS G12C \[50\]. After sotorasib, *EGFR* and *FGFR2* alterations and *NRAS*, *BRAF* and *MRAS* hotspot mutations emerged \[84\]. Overall, RTK–RAS pathway alterations other than in *KRAS* were found in 14 of 38 patients (37%) after adagrasib \[50\]. In addition, 2 of the 10 patients with a repeat tissue biopsy at progression (9 of whom had NSCLC) showed transformation from adenocarcinoma to squamous cell carcinoma without an identifiable genomic mechanism, suggesting that non-genetic lineage changes can reduce dependence on KRAS \[50\].

**5.3 Combination strategies and circulating tumour DNA monitoring**

Combinations can be matched to resistance mechanisms (Figure 4, Supplementary Table S3) \[21,22\]. Adding an anti-EGFR antibody to a direct RAS inhibitor is a standard option in chemorefractory colorectal cancer (sotorasib plus panitumumab) \[63,71\], although adagrasib plus cetuximab did not improve PFS or OS over second-line chemotherapy \[64\], and EGFR co-inhibition is being evaluated in NSCLC (sotorasib plus afatinib in the CodeBreaK 101 platform trial, NCT04185883). SHP2 inhibitors (such as TNO155 or RMC-4630) or SOS1 inhibitors (such as BI 1701963) aim to prevent compensatory nucleotide exchange \[21,22\], a MET inhibitor has been proposed for acquired MET amplification \[22\], and preclinical data support switching to a RAS(ON) inhibitor when resistance is driven by secondary KRAS alterations or increased RAS–GTP loading \[87,88\]. For downstream bypass, G12C inhibitors are being combined with MEK, RAF–MEK or mTOR inhibitors (Supplementary Table S3).

Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) offers a minimally invasive way to monitor response and resistance \[89–91\]. Detectable *KRAS* G12C ctDNA before sotorasib was associated with shorter PFS and OS \[91\], and baseline ctDNA tumour fraction with disease burden in patients treated with divarasib \[90\]. In a prospective cohort of 32 patients treated with sotorasib, early clearance of *KRAS* G12C ctDNA was associated with a higher response rate (80% versus 8%) and longer PFS (HR 0.15) and OS (HR 0.09) \[89\]; because clearance and response were assessed over the same period, the association with response is not independent evidence that clearance predicts benefit. Serial ctDNA can detect emerging resistance alterations such as *KRAS* amplification \[91\], and a rise in *KRAS* G12C allele fraction preceded radiological progression in 70% of evaluable patients \[89\]. Whether changing treatment on the basis of ctDNA improves outcomes has not been tested prospectively.

**6. Conclusions and future perspectives**

**6.1 Medicinal chemistry and clinical refinement of direct RAS inhibitors**

Several advances could broaden and prolong RAS inhibition. Inhibitors for non-G12C alleles are a priority, because *KRAS* G12D and G12V, and G12R in pancreatic cancer, together far outnumber G12C \[1,29\], and the results with daraxonrasib in pancreatic cancer show that a multi-RAS(ON) tri-complex inhibitor can improve survival across diverse *KRAS* mutations \[59\]. Pan-RAS inhibitors that also target wild-type isoforms could pre-empt compensatory feedback through untargeted paralogues, provided that the therapeutic index can be maintained through scheduling or tumour-specific delivery \[56\], and targeted protein degraders (PROTACs and molecular glues) could eliminate both catalytic and scaffolding functions of RAS \[58,92,93\].

Moving direct RAS inhibitors into first-line therapy is the most immediate opportunity. In advanced NSCLC, phase III trials are evaluating sotorasib (NCT05920356), adagrasib (KRYSTAL-7, NCT04613596), divarasib (Krascendo 2, NCT06793215) and olomorasib (SUNRAY-01, NCT06119581) with platinum doublet chemotherapy or anti-PD-(L)1 immunotherapy as first-line treatment, building on phase I and II data for these combinations \[79,94\]. These trials will need to manage overlapping toxicities, particularly hepatotoxicity with checkpoint inhibitors \[76,78\], and show meaningful survival gains over standard chemo-immunotherapy.

CNS activity matters particularly in NSCLC, where CNS metastases are present at diagnosis in 27% to 42% of patients with *KRAS* G12C mutations \[51\]. In a phase Ib cohort of the KRYSTAL-1 trial, adagrasib monotherapy achieved an intracranial ORR of 42% (95% CI 20.3%–66.5%) and a median intracranial PFS of 5.4 months in patients with untreated CNS metastases \[51\]. In a post hoc analysis of CodeBreaK 200, sotorasib prolonged CNS PFS compared with docetaxel in patients with treated, stable brain metastases (median 9.6 versus 4.5 months; HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.20–0.92) \[95\]. Olomorasib has shown intracranial responses in untreated brain metastases \[53\], and intracranial response is a secondary endpoint of SUNRAY-01.

**6.2 Clinical trial design and addressing crossover confounding**

To avoid the interpretive problems of CodeBreaK 200 and KRYSTAL-12, trials of targeted agents in pretreated disease should pre-specify how crossover and subsequent therapy will be handled in the analysis of OS. When crossover is permitted, or effective treatment is available off-study, intention-to-treat analyses bias OS differences towards the null, and censoring patients at crossover introduces selection bias. Statistical analysis plans can pre-specify established adjustment methods, such as the rank-preserving structural failure time model, the two-stage accelerated failure time model or inverse probability of censoring weighting, each with its own assumptions, as sensitivity analyses alongside the intention-to-treat result. KRYSTAL-10 and RASolute 302 also adopted dual primary endpoints of PFS and OS \[59,64\].

Trials are also moving direct RAS inhibitors into resectable disease. Krascendo 3 (NCT07541170) compares adjuvant divarasib with immunotherapy or observation in resected stage II–III *KRAS* G12C-mutant NSCLC, and RASolute 304 (NCT07252232) compares adjuvant daraxonrasib with observation after perioperative chemotherapy in resected pancreatic cancer. Such trials should build on, rather than replace, established curative treatment, and ctDNA, which tracks sotorasib response in advanced disease \[89,91\], may help to select patients. Because severe hepatotoxicity is more frequent when sotorasib closely follows anti-PD-(L)1 therapy, Chour and colleagues suggest not starting sotorasib within 30 days of the last anti-PD-(L)1 infusion \[78\], a consideration for sequencing after perioperative immunotherapy.

Accelerated approval brought sotorasib and adagrasib to patients early on the basis of response rate and duration \[7–9\]. The subsequent difficulty of confirming benefit in phase III trials, including the FDA's decision not to grant sotorasib full approval and the withdrawal, at the sponsor's request, of the adagrasib-plus-cetuximab indication in colorectal cancer, shows that accelerated approvals need robust, feasible confirmatory trials under way at the time of approval \[60,64\].

**6.3 Global implementation and translational outlook**

Extended *RAS* testing became standard in metastatic colorectal cancer once *KRAS* and *NRAS* mutations were shown to predict lack of benefit from anti-EGFR antibodies \[96,97\], and ESMO guidelines recommend molecular profiling in pancreatic cancer \[98\] and NSCLC, including resectable disease \[99\]. Broad next-generation sequencing panels also capture co-alterations, such as in *STK11*, *KEAP1* and *TP53*, that are associated with outcomes on KRAS G12C inhibitors \[40,41\], and adding plasma ctDNA testing to tissue sequencing can shorten turnaround times and may capture tumour heterogeneity \[89,91\]. As allele-specific and pan-RAS inhibitors multiply, test reports will need to state the specific allele and relevant co-mutations rather than only *KRAS* mutation status.

Access remains uneven. Newer targeted anticancer medicines are much less available, and more often paid for out of pocket, in low- and lower-middle-income countries than in high-income countries \[100\], and registered trials of directly RAS-targeted agents are concentrated in North America, East Asia and Europe (Supplementary Figure S2c). Tiered pricing, licensing agreements, simpler assays for common alleles and trials in under-represented regions could improve access.

Other modalities are being developed against oncogenic RAS: lymph-node-targeted amphiphile mutant-KRAS peptide vaccines (such as the AMPLIFY platform) that elicit mutant KRAS-specific T-cell responses in patients with minimal residual disease after curative-intent treatment \[101\], T-cell receptor gene and cell therapies directed against mutant KRAS neoepitopes \[102,103\], and generative protein-design methods such as RFdiffusion, which can design binders to difficult protein surfaces de novo \[104\].
