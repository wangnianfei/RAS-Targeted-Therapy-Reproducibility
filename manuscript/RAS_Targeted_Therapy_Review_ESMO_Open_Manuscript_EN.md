**Highlights**

- Direct RAS inhibitors now target both inactive (OFF) and active (ON) RAS.

- Divarasib improved OS over sotorasib or adagrasib in pretreated KRAS G12C NSCLC.

- Daraxonrasib roughly doubled median OS versus chemotherapy in pancreatic cancer.

- Crossover may have diluted OS differences in pretreated KRAS G12C NSCLC trials.

- Resistance: secondary KRAS mutations, RTK/MAPK bypass, histological transformation.

**Abstract**

**Background:** Direct inhibition of RAS oncoproteins, long considered intractable, has been achieved with covalent inhibitors that bind the switch-II pocket of inactive KRAS G12C and with tri-complex inhibitors that bind active RAS together with cyclophilin A.

**Methods & Evidence Synthesis:** We review the structural basis of these drug classes and the results of seven phase III randomised trials in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), colorectal cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and melanoma. We also analyse 1,151 RAS-related interventional oncology trials in ClinicalTrials.gov, 367 of which test a directly RAS-targeted agent in solid tumours.

**Key Findings:** RAS-pathway inhibitors improved the objective response rate in all seven trials and progression-free survival (PFS) in six. Overall survival (OS) was improved by divarasib over first-generation KRAS G12C inhibitors in pretreated NSCLC and by the multi-RAS(ON) inhibitor daraxonrasib over chemotherapy in pancreatic cancer (median 13.2 versus 6.7 months). Sotorasib did not improve OS over docetaxel, a comparison complicated by crossover, and OS data for adagrasib remain immature. In colorectal cancer, KRAS G12C inhibitors need EGFR co-blockade: sotorasib plus panitumumab improved PFS in refractory disease, whereas adagrasib plus cetuximab did not improve PFS or OS over second-line chemotherapy, and its US accelerated approval was withdrawn at the sponsor's request. Acquired resistance involves secondary *KRAS* alterations, receptor tyrosine kinase and MAPK-pathway bypass and histological transformation, and *STK11* and *KEAP1* co-mutations are associated with poorer outcomes.

**Conclusions:** Priorities include inhibitors for non-G12C alleles, first-line and perioperative trials, combinations matched to resistance mechanisms, and trial designs that pre-specify how crossover will be handled in the analysis of OS.

**Keywords:** RAS-targeted therapy; KRAS inhibitors; Randomised controlled trials; Drug resistance; Precision oncology

**1. Introduction: direct RAS targeting in solid tumours**

Activating mutations in RAS genes are among the most common oncogenic drivers in human cancer: about 19% of patients with cancer carry a RAS mutation, corresponding to roughly 3.4 million new cases a year worldwide \[1\]. For nearly four decades after *HRAS*, *KRAS* and *NRAS* were identified as transforming oncogenes in the early 1980s \[2–4\], RAS proteins were considered undruggable. Their picomolar affinity for guanine nucleotides and the lack of deep pockets on the G-domain surface defeated conventional medicinal chemistry.

This changed in 2013, when Ostrem and colleagues described an inducible pocket beneath switch II (the switch-II pocket, S-IIP) in GDP-bound KRAS(G12C) \[5\]. Covalent inhibitors that bind this pocket trap KRAS(G12C) in its inactive state without affecting wild-type RAS \[5,6\]. Accelerated approvals of sotorasib (AMG 510) in 2021 and adagrasib (MRTX849) in 2022 for previously treated *KRAS* G12C-mutant advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) showed that RAS can be inhibited directly in patients \[7–9\]. The field has since expanded to second-generation covalent G12C inhibitors (divarasib, olomorasib, glecirasib), non-covalent inhibitors of other alleles such as KRAS G12D (MRTX1133), and tri-complex inhibitors of active RAS that act together with cyclophilin A (daraxonrasib, RMC-6236) \[10–14\], as well as allosteric inhibitors of the exchange factor SOS1 \[15,16\].

We first summarise the structural biology, nucleotide cycling and allele epidemiology of RAS and the mechanisms of current drug classes (Figure 1). We then compare the phase III randomised evidence across disease settings (Table 1, Figure 3) and review registered trials (Figure 4), mechanisms of acquired resistance (Figure 5) and future directions. Throughout, we distinguish what randomised trials have shown from what remains to be confirmed.

**2. Biology and epidemiology of the RAS pathway in solid tumours**

**2.1 The binary GTPase cycle and structural basis of activation**

RAS proteins operate as binary molecular switches regulated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) and GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs). The human RAS family comprises three genes (*KRAS*, *NRAS*, and *HRAS*) encoding conserved 21-kDa small GTPases \[2\]. Each isoform contains a catalytic G-domain (residues 1–166) and a flexible C-terminal hypervariable region that undergoes CAAX-directed farnesylation and carboxymethylation for plasma membrane anchoring \[2\]. The G-domain houses two dynamic loop segments, designated switch I (residues 30–38) and switch II (residues 59–76), which govern nucleotide binding and effector engagement (Figure 1a) \[17\]. In physiological conditions, SOS1 and SOS2 catalyse the dissociation of guanosine diphosphate (GDP), permitting the rapid binding of abundant intracellular guanosine triphosphate (GTP) and inducing a rigid, closed conformation that binds downstream effectors \[2,18,19\]. Conversely, GAPs such as NF1 accelerate intrinsic GTP hydrolysis by more than five orders of magnitude (&gt;10^5-fold) through insertion of an 'arginine finger' into the catalytic site, restoring the inactive GDP-bound state \[20\]. Oncogenic missense mutations at codons 12, 13, or 61 sterically impede GAP insertion and abolish intrinsic GTP hydrolysis, freezing the protein in a constitutive, GTP-bound oncogenic state.

Active GTP-bound RAS transmits downstream signals primarily through the RAF-MEK-ERK (MAPK) and PI3K-AKT-mTOR cascades, alongside secondary effectors such as RALGDS, PLCepsilon, and pro-apoptotic RASSF1A \[2,3,21\]. Under physiological conditions, active ERK phosphorylates upstream receptor tyrosine kinases, SOS1, and CRAF, establishing potent negative feedback loops that attenuate pathway flux \[21,22\]. Inhibiting RAS or its effectors relieves this feedback and leads to rebound activation of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) such as EGFR, MET and HER2, which promotes resistance (Section 5.2) \[22,23\].

**2.2 Molecular epidemiology and allele-specific landscape across solid tumours**

Observed RAS mutation frequencies vary with clinical stage, registry composition, and tissue sampling timing. In The Cancer Genome Atlas collection of 10,967 resected primary tumours across 32 cancer types, the overall RAS mutation rate is 10.7% \[24\]. By contrast, clinical sequencing panels profiling advanced or metastatic solid tumours consistently report overall frequencies of 17% to 22%, reflecting the aggressive biological behaviour and metastatic propensity of RAS-driven tumours \[1,25\]. Across the three isoforms, *KRAS* accounts for approximately 75% of all RAS alterations, followed by *NRAS* (17%) and *HRAS* (8%) \[1\].

The organ-specific distribution of RAS mutations reflects both tissue-specific expression patterns and distinct biochemical properties of individual mutant alleles. *KRAS* alterations predominate in adenocarcinomas of the gastrointestinal tract and lungs, occurring in approximately 90% of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas, 45% to 50% of colorectal carcinomas, and 30% to 35% of lung adenocarcinomas \[1,25\]; in a large tissue-based clinical sequencing cohort the corresponding frequencies were lower for pancreatic cancer (73.5%), probably reflecting the low tumour cellularity of pancreatic specimens (Figure 2a) \[26\]. *NRAS* mutations are primarily observed in cutaneous melanoma (20%–25%), acute myeloid leukaemia (10%–15%), and thyroid carcinoma \[1,25\]. *HRAS* mutations are less frequent overall (1%–2%) but enriched in head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, bladder urothelial cancers, and salivary gland tumours \[1,27\]. Codon-12 and codon-13 mutations disrupt the GAP-binding interface through steric clash, whereas codon-61 mutations directly disorganise the catalytic water molecule required for GTP hydrolysis, conferring higher baseline GTP loading and stronger oncogenic potency.

Allele distributions differ substantially across tumour types, shaped by tissue lineage biology and environmental exposures. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, *KRAS* G12D accounts for roughly 40% of mutations, followed by G12V (32%), G12R (16%), and rarely G12C (1%–2%) (Figure 2b) \[28,29\]. In colorectal cancer, G12D (30%) and G12V (23%) similarly predominate, whereas G12C represents approximately 8% to 10% of cases \[1,30\]. By contrast, non-small cell lung cancer exhibits a distinctive tobacco carcinogen signature (G-to-T transversions) that makes G12C the predominant variant (roughly 40% of *KRAS* mutations), followed by G12V (18%) and G12D (14%) \[1,31–33\]. In cutaneous melanoma, *NRAS* codon-61 mutations (predominantly Q61R and Q61K) represent roughly 80% of all RAS alterations, with *KRAS* and *HRAS* rarely mutated \[1,25\].

Co-occurring alterations add biological and clinical heterogeneity. In *KRAS*-mutant NSCLC, frequent co-mutations involve *TP53* (about 40%), *STK11* (about 30%) and *KEAP1* (20%–25%), and these define distinct subgroups \[34,35\]. Tumours with co-mutated *KRAS* and *TP53* (KP) tend to have a high tumour mutational burden, immune infiltration and sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors \[34,36\]. Tumours with co-mutated *KRAS* and *STK11* (KL) show STING silencing, an immune-cold microenvironment and primary resistance to anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy \[35,37,38\]. *KEAP1* loss, which often co-occurs with *STK11* loss, activates NRF2 and is associated with shorter benefit from KRAS G12C inhibitors \[39,40\].

**3. Biophysical barriers and direct pharmacological targeting strategies**

**3.1 Overcoming the 'undruggable' paradigm through switch-II pocket discovery**

Direct targeting of RAS was hindered for decades by three biophysical obstacles. First, the binding affinity of RAS for GDP and GTP is extraordinarily high, with dissociation constants in the picomolar range (Kd ~10^-11 to 10^-12 M), while intracellular GTP concentrations are abundant (0.1–0.5 mM) \[3,41\]. This nucleotide-binding affinity rendered competitive active-site inhibition unfeasible with conventional small molecules, in sharp contrast to protein kinase inhibitors that compete with micromolar-affinity ATP \[41\]. Second, the surface of the RAS G-domain is remarkably smooth and rigid, lacking the deep, hydrophobic clefts typical of druggable enzyme active sites \[3,41\]. Third, the structural divergence between active GTP-bound and inactive GDP-bound states is largely confined to the two dynamic switch regions, meaning that compounds targeting the inactive state must compete with exchange factors that rapidly recharge the protein with GTP \[6\].

Early strategies that bypassed direct nucleotide competition failed to deliver meaningful clinical benefit. Farnesyltransferase inhibitors (such as tipifarnib) sought to block post-translational CAAX lipid modification and displace RAS from the membrane; however, KRAS and NRAS utilised alternative prenylation by geranylgeranyltransferase-I, escaping inhibition in clinical trials \[3,42\]. Similarly, targeting downstream effector kinases (RAF, MEK, ERK, or PI3K) with single-agent inhibitors produced modest efficacy curtailed by rapid feedback pathway reactivation and dose-limiting toxicities arising from the inhibition of essential signalling in normal tissues \[43–45\].

The identification of the switch-II allosteric pocket in 2013 enabled direct RAS inhibition. Ostrem and Shokat solved crystal structures of GDP-bound KRAS(G12C), revealing an inducible, shallow hydrophobic cavity (the S-IIP) between switch II and the central beta-sheet (beta1–beta3) that is absent in active GTP-bound RAS (Figure 1a) \[5\]. Occupancy of the switch-II pocket by an allosteric small molecule locks switch I and switch II in an inactive conformation, shifting the nucleotide-exchange equilibrium to favour the GDP-bound state and abolishing effector binding \[5,46\]. Crucially, while oncogenic G12D or G12V mutations abolish intrinsic GTP hydrolysis, the mutant cysteine in KRAS(G12C) retains residual intrinsic GTPase activity, enabling the protein to cycle through the transient, GDP-bound state where it can be covalently trapped by S-IIP small molecules \[6,47\].

**3.2 Four contemporary mechanistic classes of direct RAS inhibitors**

The first mechanistic class comprises allele-specific, GDP-bound (OFF-state) covalent inhibitors designed specifically for KRAS(G12C) (Figure 1a, detailed in Supplementary Table S1). Sotorasib and adagrasib bind within the switch-II pocket and form a covalent adduct with mutant Cys12, yet they possess distinct chemical scaffolds and pocket-engagement topologies \[48,49\]. Sotorasib features an acrylamide-substituted quinazolinone core that projects into an induced subpocket bounded by residues His95, Tyr96, and Gln99 without forming strong, direct interactions with His95 \[49\]. Consequently, secondary mutations at His95 (such as H95D, H95Q, or H95R) generally preserve sotorasib binding, whereas adagrasib forms direct hydrogen-bonding and van der Waals contacts with both His95 and Tyr96 \[49\]. Adagrasib also exhibits a considerably longer terminal elimination half-life (approximately 23 hours versus 5 hours for sotorasib), a larger volume of distribution, and confirmed blood-brain barrier penetration with durable intracranial activity \[9,50\].

Second-generation covalent G12C inhibitors were designed for greater potency, selectivity and tissue distribution (Supplementary Table S1). Divarasib, for example, is 5 to 20 times as potent and up to 50 times as selective in vitro as sotorasib and adagrasib \[10,51\], and its faster covalent engagement suppresses ERK phosphorylation at lower concentrations \[51\]. Furthermore, olomorasib produced intracranial responses in patients with untreated, active brain metastases in its first-in-human study, a relevant finding because the central nervous system is a common site of progression \[52\].

The second mechanistic class comprises non-covalent inhibitors, which extend direct inhibition beyond G12C to alleles that lack a reactive cysteine (Supplementary Table S1). *KRAS* G12D, the most frequent oncogenic variant in human cancer, features an aspartic acid residue whose carboxylate side chain lacks the nucleophilicity of cysteine, precluding conventional acrylamide-based covalent tethering \[12\]. Early non-covalent inhibitors such as MRTX1133 occupy the switch-II pocket through a tight network of ionic, hydrogen-bonding, and salt-bridge interactions with Asp12 and surrounding residues \[12\]. MRTX1133 demonstrates sub-nanomolar affinity for GDP-bound KRAS(G12D) and selective antitumor activity in preclinical models of pancreatic and colorectal cancer \[12\]. Other non-covalent compounds, such as BI-2852, bind a pocket between switch I and switch II and are not allele-selective \[53\]. However, because non-covalent inhibitors do not form an irreversible adduct, their target occupancy depends continuously on systemic drug exposure, making sustained pharmacokinetic coverage essential to prevent rapid pathway reactivation.

The third mechanistic class comprises tri-complex inhibitors of the active, GTP-bound (ON-state) conformation (Figure 1b, Supplementary Table S1). These small molecules first bind the abundant chaperone cyclophilin A (CYPA), creating a composite binary interface; the drug–CYPA complex then binds GTP-bound RAS next to switch I and switch II, sterically blocking effector engagement (such as RAF binding) without requiring displacement of bound GTP \[13,54\]. Daraxonrasib (RMC-6236) and its preclinical analogue RMC-7977 bind non-covalently and, because the binding interface is broadly conserved, inhibit multiple active RAS isoforms (KRAS, NRAS and HRAS), diverse codon-12 and codon-13 variants and wild-type RAS \[13,55\]. Mutant-selective tri-complex inhibitors additionally form a covalent bond with the mutant residue: elironrasib (RMC-6291) with Cys12 of KRAS G12C \[54\] and zoldonrasib (RMC-9805) with Asp12 of KRAS G12D, which conventional acrylamide chemistry cannot target \[56\]. Because multi-selective inhibitors also suppress wild-type RAS activity in healthy tissues, their therapeutic window depends on careful dose selection \[55\].

The fourth mechanistic class comprises targeted degraders, heterobifunctional molecules that bind KRAS and recruit an E3 ubiquitin ligase to induce proteasomal degradation of the protein (Supplementary Table S1); the pan-KRAS degrader ACBI3, for example, suppressed pathway signalling more durably than inhibition in preclinical models \[57\], and clinical data are discussed in Section 6.1. Allosteric inhibitors of SOS1 (such as BI 1701963 and MRTX0902) are not direct RAS inhibitors: they bind the catalytic pocket of SOS1, blocking its interaction with RAS and preventing GEF-mediated nucleotide exchange \[15,16\], and, like SHP2 inhibitors, they are being developed mainly as combination partners (Section 5.3).

**4.2 Second-line monotherapy in non-small cell lung cancer: CodeBreaK 200, KRYSTAL-12, and Krascendo 1**

In advanced *KRAS* G12C NSCLC progressing after platinum chemotherapy and anti-PD-(L)1 therapy, two phase III trials compared oral G12C monotherapy with intravenous docetaxel (Table 1, Figure 3a). In the CodeBreaK 200 trial, 345 patients were randomised to sotorasib (960 mg daily) or docetaxel (75 mg/m2 every 3 weeks) \[59\]. Sotorasib met its primary endpoint, demonstrating a statistically significant improvement in progression-free survival by blinded independent central review (median 5.6 versus 4.5 months; hazard ratio \[HR\] 0.66, 95% confidence interval \[CI\] 0.51–0.86; p = 0.0017). Objective response rate was more than doubled (28.1% versus 13.2%; p &lt; 0.001), and disease control rate reached 82.5% versus 60.3%.

However, overall survival did not differ between the arms (median 10.6 versus 11.3 months; HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.77–1.33; p = 0.53; Figure 3b) \[59\]. Crossover probably contributed: 26% of patients in the docetaxel arm crossed over to sotorasib after progression, and 34% received a KRAS G12C inhibitor at some point \[65\]. The open-label design was a further limitation: 13% of patients assigned to docetaxel (23 of 174) were never treated, compared with 1% (2 of 171) assigned to sotorasib \[59\]. Together with a median PFS gain of about five weeks, these issues led the FDA Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee in October 2023 to question whether the PFS result could be reliably interpreted, and sotorasib did not receive full approval \[65\].

The phase III KRYSTAL-12 trial evaluated adagrasib against docetaxel in 453 patients with pretreated *KRAS* G12C NSCLC, confirming the clinical activity of direct G12C inhibition (Table 1, Figure 3a) \[60\]. Adagrasib demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in PFS over docetaxel (median 5.5 versus 3.8 months; HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.45–0.76; p &lt; 0.0001). ORR was 31.9% for adagrasib versus 9.2% for docetaxel (odds ratio 4.68; p &lt; 0.0001), with a median response duration of 8.3 months compared with 5.4 months.

Overall survival data from KRYSTAL-12 were immature at the time of the primary report \[60\], and no final overall survival analysis had been published by the cut-off date of this review. After a protocol amendment requested by the FDA, patients in the docetaxel arm were allowed to cross over to adagrasib at disease progression, and about 29% did so \[66\], which may attenuate any eventual survival difference. Adagrasib also has intracranial activity, consistent with its penetration of the blood–brain barrier in preclinical models \[50\]. In the phase II KRYSTAL-1 trial, adagrasib monotherapy achieved an intracranial ORR of 42% (95% CI 20.3%–66.5%) and a median intracranial PFS of 5.4 months in patients with untreated CNS metastases \[9,50\], findings relevant to the management of brain metastases \[68\].

The randomised phase III Krascendo 1 trial provided the first head-to-head comparison between direct RAS inhibitors. It evaluated the second-generation covalent inhibitor divarasib (400 mg once daily) against investigator's choice of a first-generation KRAS G12C inhibitor (sotorasib or adagrasib) in 338 patients with previously treated KRAS G12C-mutant advanced NSCLC (167 versus 171 patients; 156 control patients received sotorasib and 15 received adagrasib) (Table 1, Figure 3) \[61\]. Divarasib was designed for greater switch-II pocket complementarity and is approximately 5- to 20-fold more potent than first-generation compounds in vitro \[10,51\]. Divarasib met its primary endpoint, significantly prolonging PFS by blinded independent central review (median 9.8 versus 5.7 months; HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.76; p &lt; 0.001), and increased the confirmed ORR (58.4% versus 32.3%), with a longer median duration of response (11.9 versus 6.9 months) \[61\]. At the first interim analysis, divarasib also significantly prolonged overall survival (median 17.7 versus 12.0 months; HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.41–0.78; p &lt; 0.001), and grade 3 or 4 treatment-related adverse events were less frequent (24.6% versus 34.5%) \[61\]. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that deeper and more sustained target inhibition improves clinical outcomes, although the trial was not designed to test this mechanism. Because the control arm received an active KRAS G12C inhibitor rather than docetaxel, the survival advantage cannot be extrapolated to a comparison with chemotherapy.

**4.3 Colorectal cancer and the biological requirement for vertical EGFR co-inhibition**

KRAS G12C inhibitor monotherapy has limited activity in metastatic colorectal cancer, with response rates of about 10% to 20% and median PFS of 4 to 6 months \[69,70\]. This reflects EGFR-dependent feedback in colorectal cancer cells \[23\]: inhibition of KRAS(G12C) suppresses ERK phosphorylation, which relieves negative feedback on EGFR, SOS1 and CRAF, and EGFR signalling then rapidly reactivates wild-type and mutant RAS (Section 5.2) \[23\]. Combining a G12C inhibitor with an anti-EGFR antibody (cetuximab or panitumumab) is intended to block both the driver and this feedback (Table 1, Figure 3a) \[62,70,71\].

The phase III CodeBreaK 300 trial provided randomised evidence for this approach, evaluating sotorasib plus panitumumab in 160 patients with chemorefractory *KRAS* G12C mCRC (Table 1, Figure 3a) \[62\]. Patients were randomised to sotorasib 960 mg plus panitumumab, sotorasib 240 mg plus panitumumab, or investigator's choice of standard therapy (trifluridine-tipiracil or regorafenib). Both sotorasib doses combined with panitumumab significantly prolonged PFS compared with standard therapy: median PFS was 5.6 months in the 960-mg group (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.30–0.80; p = 0.006) and 3.9 months in the 240-mg group (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.36–0.93; p = 0.03), versus 2.2 months for standard therapy. Confirmed ORR was 26.4% (960 mg) and 5.7% (240 mg), compared with 0% in the control arm (p &lt; 0.001).

The phase III KRYSTAL-10 trial evaluated whether targeted dual blockade could replace cytotoxic doublet chemotherapy in second-line colorectal cancer. The trial randomised 461 patients with KRAS G12C-mutant mCRC progressing after first-line therapy to adagrasib plus cetuximab (231 patients) or investigator's choice of FOLFIRI or mFOLFOX6 with or without a VEGF/VEGFR inhibitor (230 patients), with PFS by blinded independent central review and OS as dual primary endpoints (Table 1, Figure 3) \[63\]. Although the combination roughly tripled the objective response rate (47% versus 16%), neither primary endpoint was met: median PFS was 7.5 versus 8.1 months (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.71–1.13; p = 0.32; Figure 3a) and median OS was 21.6 versus 21.7 months (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.67–1.03; p = 0.09; Figure 3b) \[63\]. Grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events occurred in 46% versus 55% of patients, and 3% versus 2% discontinued treatment because of them \[63\]. The higher response rate therefore did not translate into longer disease control than an active second-line chemotherapy doublet. Following this result, the accelerated-approval indication for adagrasib plus cetuximab in previously treated KRAS G12C-mutant mCRC was withdrawn at the sponsor's request, as recorded by the US FDA on 1 September 2026 \[72\] (Supplementary Table S2).

**4.4 Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma: breakthrough with multi-RAS(ON) inhibition**

The phase III RASolute 302 trial provided randomised validation of multi-RAS(ON) tri-complex inhibition in metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, where more than 90% of tumours harbour oncogenic KRAS alterations (predominantly G12D, G12V, and G12R) that are not targetable by G12C covalent inhibitors (Table 1, Figure 3) \[58,73,74\]. RASolute 302 randomised 500 patients with previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer 1:1 to daraxonrasib (RMC-6236, 300 mg once daily; 248 patients) or investigator's choice of chemotherapy (gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, modified FOLFIRINOX, FOLFOX, or liposomal irinotecan plus fluorouracil and leucovorin; 252 patients); crossover was not permitted \[58\]. The dual primary endpoints were OS and PFS in patients with RAS G12 mutations (459 patients, 91.8%). At the first interim analysis, daraxonrasib significantly prolonged OS in this population (median 13.2 versus 6.6 months; HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.30–0.54; p &lt; 0.001) and in the overall population (median 13.2 versus 6.7 months; HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.30–0.53; p &lt; 0.001; Figure 3b) \[58\]. PFS by blinded independent central review was approximately doubled (RAS G12 population: median 7.3 versus 3.5 months; HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.34–0.59; overall population: 7.2 versus 3.6 months; HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.38–0.64; both p &lt; 0.001; Figure 3a), and the objective response rate in the overall population was 31.6% versus 11.2% \[58\]. Grade 3 or higher adverse events occurred in 61.8% versus 69.6% of patients, and treatment-related adverse events led to discontinuation in 1.2% versus 11.2% \[58\]. Unlike the pretreated NSCLC and colorectal cancer trials, RASolute 302 did not permit crossover, and few effective later-line options exist in pancreatic cancer; both factors probably helped the PFS benefit translate into an OS benefit. Of note, 84.9% of patients assigned to chemotherapy received any trial treatment, compared with 97.2% of those assigned to daraxonrasib \[58\].

**4.5 Cutaneous melanoma and NRAS-mutant disease: lessons from the NEMO trial**

The clinical utility of pathway inhibition in *NRAS*-mutant solid tumours was evaluated in the randomised phase III NEMO trial, which compared the MEK inhibitor binimetinib (45 mg twice daily) with dacarbazine (1,000 mg/m2 every 3 weeks) in 402 patients with advanced *NRAS*-mutant cutaneous melanoma (Table 1, Figure 3) \[64\]. Binimetinib significantly improved progression-free survival over dacarbazine (median 2.8 versus 1.5 months; HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.47–0.80; p &lt; 0.001) and increased ORR (15% versus 7%), but did not prolong overall survival (median 11.0 versus 10.1 months; HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.75–1.33; p = 0.99; Figure 3b) \[64\]. Crossover to binimetinib was not permitted, but subsequent immunotherapy in both arms may have diluted any survival difference. The modest PFS gain of 1.3 months is consistent with the limited durability of MEK inhibitor monotherapy described in Section 3.1.

**4.6 Safety profiles and toxicity management across drug classes**

Cross-trial comparisons of safety must distinguish treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) from all-cause treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and account for differences in treatment duration. In the two phase III trials against docetaxel, grade 3 or higher TRAEs occurred in 33% versus 40% of patients with sotorasib \[59\] and in 47% versus 46% with adagrasib \[60\]. In a pooled analysis of 549 patients treated with sotorasib monotherapy, grade 3 or higher TRAEs occurred in 26.8%; diarrhoea, nausea and transaminase elevations were the most common, and most resolved with dose interruption or reduction \[75\]. Gastrointestinal and hepatic events also predominate with adagrasib \[9,76\].

Combinations change the toxicity profile. In CodeBreaK 300, grade 3 or higher TRAEs occurred in 35.8% of patients receiving sotorasib 960 mg plus panitumumab and in 43.1% of those receiving trifluridine–tipiracil or regorafenib; skin toxicity and hypomagnesaemia were the most common events with the combination \[62\]. Combining or sequencing covalent G12C inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors raises the risk of hepatotoxicity. In a French multicentre cohort, severe sotorasib-related hepatotoxicity occurred in 33% of patients whose last treatment before sotorasib was anti-PD-(L)1 therapy, compared with 11% of other patients \[77\]; in a pooled analysis, treatment-related hepatotoxicity occurred in 33.3% of patients who stopped immunotherapy less than 3 months before sotorasib and in 13.8% of those who stopped it earlier \[75\]. The mechanism is not established. With olomorasib plus pembrolizumab, grade 3 or higher TRAEs occurred in 33.3% of patients and led to discontinuation of both drugs in 6.5% \[78\].

**5. Clinical trial landscape, resistance networks, and translational frontiers**

**5.1 Registered clinical trials of directly RAS-targeted agents**

We queried ClinicalTrials.gov (search cut-off date: 18 November 2026) for interventional oncology trials that name a RAS gene or a RAS-directed agent and retrieved 1,182 records first posted between 2000 and 2026. Each record was classified by its relation to RAS with explicit rules, and the classification was then checked record by record against the registered intervention descriptions. This review removed 31 records that were not oncology trials in patients with cancer (23 pharmacology studies in healthy volunteers or participants with organ impairment and 8 non-oncology studies) and corrected the classification of 57 trials; all decisions and their reasons are listed in the study repository. Of the remaining 1,151 trials, 369 (32.1%) test a directly RAS-targeted agent, 130 test inhibitors of RAS regulators such as SHP2 or SOS1, 607 select or stratify patients by RAS status without a RAS-targeted agent, and 45 only mention RAS (Figure 4). The analyses below refer to the 367 directly RAS-targeted trials in patients with solid tumours; two vaccine trials restricted to haematological malignancies are excluded. Of these, 133 (36.2%) are phase 1, 103 (28.1%) phase 1/2, 88 (24.0%) phase 2 and 43 (11.7%) phase 2/3 or 3 (Figure 4a). KRAS G12C inhibitors are the most common drug class (201 trials), followed by KRAS G12D inhibitors (60), RAS vaccines, T-cell receptor therapies or T-cell engagers (57) and multi-RAS(ON) or pan-RAS inhibitors (35) (Figure 4b). The United States hosts at least one site in 206 trials (56.1%) and mainland China in 152 (41.4%); 104 and 110 trials, respectively, are conducted entirely within these two countries. By region, 210 trials have sites in North America, 188 in East Asia, 93 in Europe and 80 elsewhere, and 117 trials (31.9%) are multinational (Figure 4c). A trial may list several tumour types: histology-agnostic advanced solid tumours are listed in 152 trials (41.4%), non-small cell lung cancer in 149 (40.6%), pancreatic cancer in 105 (28.6%) and colorectal cancer in 80 (21.8%).

Registration activity has shifted towards direct RAS inhibitors since the switch-II pocket was described in 2013 \[5\]. Directly RAS-targeted trials accounted for 22 of 484 RAS-related records (4.5%) first posted before 2018, 107 of 210 (51.0%) in 2021–2023 and 209 of 313 (66.8%) in 2024–2026; 316 of the 367 directly RAS-targeted trials (86.1%) were first posted in 2021 or later. Most of these trials (256; 69.8%) register more than one intervention. Because registered interventions include control-arm agents, this proportion overstates the number of trials testing combination regimens.

The design of registration trials has evolved in response to methodological lessons from earlier studies. Early phase III trials (such as CodeBreaK 200) employed conventional two-arm randomised designs with docetaxel as the active control, which introduced open-label attrition bias and allowed post-progression crossover that obscured OS endpoints \[59\]. More recent trials have restricted post-progression crossover; RASolute 302, for example, did not permit it \[58\]. In addition, some phase III trials (such as KRYSTAL-10 and RASolute 302) adopted dual primary endpoints of PFS and OS, reflecting regulatory expectations for survival validation \[58,63\].

Geographic disparities in regulatory approval pathways shape the global availability of direct RAS inhibitors (Supplementary Table S2). While sotorasib and adagrasib received accelerated approval from the US FDA and conditional marketing authorisation from the European Medicines Agency (EMA), the regulatory trajectory diverged when confirmatory trials reported. After the October 2023 Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee meeting, the FDA did not grant sotorasib full approval, whereas in the European Union its conditional marketing authorisation was renewed in November 2024 with CodeBreaK 200 results added to the product information \[79\]. In China, the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) approved domestic G12C inhibitors—including garsorasib, fulzerasib, and glecirasib—under conditional approval frameworks, reflecting rapid drug development and distinct commercial ecosystems \[11,80,81\].

Industry is the lead sponsor of 256 of the 367 directly RAS-targeted trials (69.8%) and of all 43 phase 2/3 or 3 trials. Academic centres, cooperative groups and government bodies lead the remaining 111 trials (30.2%), of which 54 are phase 2 or later and 71 test a KRAS G12C inhibitor.

**5.2 Mechanisms of acquired resistance and multimodal counterstrategies**

Acquired resistance limits the duration of direct RAS inhibition, with median response durations across monotherapy trials rarely exceeding 6 to 11 months \[59,60,62\]. Genomic analyses of paired baseline and progression samples show that resistance arises through on-target *KRAS* alterations, reactivation of bypass pathways and changes in histology (Figure 5, and Supplementary Table S3 / Supplementary Figure S1) \[49,82–85\]. Among 38 patients with acquired resistance to adagrasib, switch-II pocket mutations (R68S, H95D/Q/R and Y96C) were found in 4 (11%) and any acquired KRAS alteration in 7, and these mutations directly alter drug binding (Figure 5) \[49\]; among 43 patients treated with sotorasib, treatment-emergent alterations, including in KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, EGFR and FGFR2, were found in 27 \[83\]. Adagrasib extends deeply into an induced subpocket to form direct hydrogen-bonding and aromatic interactions with His95; consequently, secondary mutations at His95 (including H95D, H95Q, and H95R) disrupt adagrasib engagement, conferring resistance to adagrasib but not to sotorasib in vitro, consistent with weaker sotorasib–His95 interactions \[49\]. However, each clinical H95 mutation co-occurred with other alterations expected to confer resistance to both drugs, so this asymmetry does not by itself support switching from adagrasib to sotorasib \[49\]. By contrast, Y96C conferred resistance to both adagrasib and sotorasib, and a Y96D mutation has also been reported after adagrasib \[49\]. R68S likewise conferred resistance to both drugs, whereas deep mutational scanning identified mutations outside the pocket that enhance nucleotide exchange (for example G13D, A59S and A146P) and conferred more modest resistance \[49\].

Off-target mechanisms bypass RAS inhibition by restoring signalling through parallel or downstream pathways (Figure 5) \[49,82,83\]. Acquired alterations upstream of KRAS include *MET* amplification, the only identified mechanism in 2 of 38 patients, and oncogenic fusions involving ALK, RET, BRAF, RAF1 and FGFR3 \[49\]; EGFR and FGFR2 alterations emerged after sotorasib \[83\]. Downstream in the MAPK cascade, acquired BRAF V600E, NRAS Q61K and MAP2K1 (MEK1) mutations (K57N, K57T and in-frame deletions) reactivate ERK signalling independently of KRAS G12C \[49\], and NRAS, BRAF and MRAS hotspot mutations were also observed after sotorasib \[83\]. Overall, RTK–RAS pathway alterations other than in KRAS were found in 14 of 38 patients (37%) after adagrasib \[49\]. Furthermore, 2 of 9 patients with lung adenocarcinoma who had paired biopsies showed histological transformation to squamous cell carcinoma without an identifiable genomic mechanism, suggesting that non-genetic lineage changes can reduce dependence on KRAS \[49\].

**5.3 Combination strategies and circulating tumour DNA monitoring**

Combination strategies can be matched to resistance mechanisms (Figure 5, Supplementary Table S3) \[21,22\]. Vertical pathway inhibition combining a direct RAS inhibitor with an anti-EGFR antibody is a standard option in chemorefractory colorectal cancer (sotorasib plus panitumumab) \[62,70\], although adagrasib plus cetuximab did not improve PFS or OS over second-line chemotherapy in KRYSTAL-10 \[63\], and EGFR co-inhibition is also being evaluated in NSCLC (for example, sotorasib plus afatinib in the CodeBreaK 101 platform trial, NCT04185883). Combining direct RAS inhibitors with SHP2 inhibitors (such as TNO155 or RMC-4630) or SOS1 inhibitors (such as BI 1701963) aims to prevent compensatory nucleotide exchange \[21,22\]. Adding a MET inhibitor has been proposed for acquired *MET* amplification \[22\], and preclinical data support switching to a RAS(ON) inhibitor when resistance is driven by secondary *KRAS* alterations or increased RAS-GTP loading \[86,87\].

Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) offers a minimally invasive way to monitor response and resistance \[88–90\]. Detectable *KRAS* G12C ctDNA before sotorasib was associated with shorter PFS and OS \[90\], and baseline ctDNA tumour fraction was associated with disease burden in patients treated with divarasib \[89\]. Early clearance of KRAS G12C ctDNA was associated with benefit: in a prospective cohort of 32 patients treated with sotorasib, the response rate was 80% with clearance versus 8% without, and clearance was associated with longer PFS (HR 0.15) and OS (HR 0.09) \[88\]. Because clearance and radiological response were assessed over the same period, the association with response rate is not independent evidence that clearance predicts benefit. Serial ctDNA can also detect emerging resistance alterations such as *KRAS* amplification \[90\], and a rise in *KRAS* G12C allele fraction preceded radiological progression in 70% of evaluable patients \[88\]. Whether changing treatment on the basis of ctDNA improves outcomes has not yet been tested prospectively.

**6. Conclusions and future perspectives**

Direct RAS inhibitors have turned a target once considered undruggable into a treatable one. Across seven pivotal phase III randomised controlled trials, direct RAS and pathway inhibitors improved objective response rates in every trial and significantly prolonged progression-free survival in six, the exception being KRYSTAL-10 in second-line colorectal cancer, across advanced non-small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, and cutaneous melanoma \[58–64\]. The same trials also expose biological and methodological problems that set the agenda for drug design and trial conduct.

**6.1 Medicinal chemistry and clinical refinement of direct RAS inhibitors**

Future progress in RAS targeting will depend on advances in chemical biology that expand the scope and durability of inhibition. First, inhibitors for non-G12C alleles are a priority, because KRAS G12D, G12V and G12R are far more common than G12C across RAS-mutant cancers \[1,28\]. The results with daraxonrasib in pancreatic cancer show that multi-RAS(ON) tri-complex inhibitors can achieve clinically meaningful survival improvements across diverse *KRAS* mutations without requiring covalent cysteine tethering \[58\]. Second, pan-RAS inhibitors that target both mutant and wild-type RAS isoforms offer a potential solution to compensatory feedback through untargeted paralogues, provided that the therapeutic index can be maintained through selective scheduling or tumour-specific delivery \[55,57\]. Third, targeted protein degraders (PROTACs and molecular glues; Section 3.2) that recruit E3 ubiquitin ligases to induce proteasomal destruction of RAS oncoproteins represent an alternative modality that could eliminate both catalytic and scaffolding functions of the protein \[91,92\].

Moving direct RAS inhibitors from the second-line and third-line settings into frontline therapy represents the most immediate opportunity to improve patient outcomes. In advanced NSCLC, ongoing phase III trials are evaluating sotorasib (NCT05920356), adagrasib (KRYSTAL-7, NCT04613596) and the second-generation G12C inhibitors divarasib (Krascendo 2, NCT06793215) and olomorasib (SUNRAY-01, NCT06119581) in combination with platinum doublet chemotherapy or anti-PD-(L)1 immunotherapy as first-line treatment \[78,93\]. Earlier use in combination might delay resistance, although this has not been shown. Trials will need to manage overlapping toxicities, particularly the hepatotoxicity observed when covalent G12C inhibitors are combined with checkpoint inhibitors \[75,77\], and ensure that targeted combinations deliver meaningful survival gains over standard-of-care chemo-immunotherapy.

Optimising central nervous system (CNS) penetration is critical for direct RAS inhibitors, particularly in NSCLC where 30% to 40% of patients develop brain metastases during the course of their disease \[50\]. Adagrasib has demonstrated clinically meaningful intracranial activity and durable CNS disease control in pretreated patients with active brain metastases \[50\], and in a post hoc analysis of CodeBreaK 200, sotorasib prolonged CNS progression-free survival compared with docetaxel in patients with treated, stable brain metastases (median 9.6 versus 4.5 months; HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.20–0.92) \[94\]. Olomorasib has shown intracranial responses in untreated brain metastases \[52\], and intracranial response rate and duration of response are secondary endpoints of the phase III SUNRAY-01 trial (NCT06119581). Achieving durable CNS disease control will be essential for establishing direct RAS inhibitors as comprehensive systemic therapies.

**6.2 Clinical trial design and addressing crossover confounding**

To avoid the interpretive problems of CodeBreaK 200 and KRYSTAL-12, trials of targeted agents in pretreated disease should pre-specify how crossover and subsequent therapy will be handled in the analysis of OS. When crossover is permitted, or effective treatment is available off-study, intention-to-treat analyses bias OS differences towards the null, and censoring patients at crossover introduces selection bias. Statistical analysis plans can pre-specify established adjustment methods, such as the rank-preserving structural failure time model, the two-stage accelerated failure time model or inverse probability of censoring weighting, each with its own assumptions, as sensitivity analyses alongside the intention-to-treat result.

Trials are also moving direct RAS inhibitors into resectable disease. Krascendo 3 (NCT07541170) compares adjuvant divarasib with immunotherapy or observation in resected stage II–III *KRAS* G12C-positive NSCLC, and RASolute 304 (NCT07252232) compares adjuvant daraxonrasib with observation after completion of perioperative chemotherapy in resected pancreatic cancer. Such trials should build on, rather than replace, established curative treatment, and ctDNA-defined molecular residual disease may help to select patients \[88,90\]. Because severe hepatotoxicity is more frequent when sotorasib closely follows anti-PD-(L)1 therapy, Chour and colleagues suggest not starting sotorasib within 30 days of the last anti-PD-(L)1 infusion \[77\], a consideration for sequencing after perioperative immunotherapy.

The regulatory trajectory of direct RAS inhibitors reflects a shifting balance between rapid access and rigorous efficacy validation. Accelerated approval pathways enabled sotorasib and adagrasib to reach patients years ahead of standard approval timelines based on response rate and response duration surrogates \[7–9\]. However, the subsequent challenges in confirming overall survival benefit in phase III trials—including the FDA's decision not to grant sotorasib full approval and the withdrawal, at the sponsor's request, of the adagrasib-plus-cetuximab indication in colorectal cancer—show that accelerated approvals must be tethered to robust, feasible confirmatory trial designs \[59,63\]. Regulators increasingly expect confirmatory trials to be under way at the time of accelerated approval.

**6.3 Global implementation and translational outlook**

Comprehensive molecular profiling forms the foundation of modern RAS-directed oncology care. Guidelines recommend broad next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels that assess both specific *KRAS*, *NRAS*, and *HRAS* mutations and concurrent alterations in *TP53*, *STK11*, *KEAP1*, and *MET* at the time of advanced cancer diagnosis \[95–98\]. Adding plasma ctDNA testing to tissue NGS can shorten turnaround times and may capture tumour heterogeneity \[88,90\]. As allele-specific and pan-RAS inhibitors multiply, test reports will need to state the specific allele and relevant co-mutations rather than only *KRAS* mutation status.

The clinical benefits of direct RAS inhibitors remain unevenly distributed globally. Newer targeted anticancer medicines are much less available, and more often paid for out of pocket, in low- and lower-middle-income countries than in high-income countries \[99\], and registered trials of directly RAS-targeted agents are concentrated in North America, East Asia and Europe (Figure 4c). Tiered pricing, licensing agreements, simpler diagnostic assays (such as droplet digital PCR for common alleles) and trials in under-represented regions could improve access.

Beyond small-molecule catalytic inhibitors and degraders, other modalities are being developed against oncogenic RAS. These include lymph-node-targeted amphiphile mutant-KRAS peptide vaccines (such as the AMPLIFY platform) that elicit functional T-cell expansions in resected disease \[100\], adoptive neoantigen T-cell receptor (TCR) gene and cell therapies specifically engineered against mutant KRAS neoepitopes \[101,102\], and generative protein-design methods such as RFdiffusion, which can design binders to difficult protein surfaces de novo \[103\].
